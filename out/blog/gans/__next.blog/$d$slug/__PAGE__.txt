4:T3c81,<p>A generative adversarial network is two networks with opposing jobs.</p>
<p>The <strong>generator</strong> turns random noise into an image. The <strong>discriminator</strong> looks at an image and outputs one number: how likely it thinks this is real.</p>
<p>Neither is told what a good image looks like. The generator never sees the training data at all — its only information about reality is the discriminator's reaction to what it produced.</p>
<h2 id="the-loop"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-loop">The loop</a></h2>
<p>Show the discriminator some real images and some generated ones, and train it to tell them apart. Then train the generator to make images the discriminator scores as real. Alternate.</p>
<p>Each improvement on one side raises the bar for the other. The discriminator finds a tell, the generator learns to hide it, the discriminator has to find a subtler one.</p>
<h2 id="where-it-is-supposed-to-end-up"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-it-is-supposed-to-end-up">Where it is supposed to end up</a></h2>
<p>At the point where the discriminator cannot do better than guessing — scoring everything around 0.5.</p>
<p>That has a consequence people find surprising: the losses have fixed target values, and lower is not better.</p>

<p>Read the first row again. A discriminator loss of 0.02 looks excellent by every normal instinct about loss curves, and it means the run is failing — the discriminator has won so completely that the generator has nothing to learn from.</p>
<p>This is the thing that makes GANs awkward to train. In ordinary training, loss going down means progress. Here both losses are measured against a moving opponent, so a falling generator loss might mean the generator improved, or might mean the discriminator got worse. The number alone cannot distinguish those.</p>

<h2 id="mode-collapse"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#mode-collapse">Mode collapse</a></h2>
<p>The characteristic failure. The generator finds one output that reliably fools the discriminator and produces only variations of it.</p>
<p>Nothing in the objective forbids this. The generator is rewarded for fooling the discriminator, and producing the same convincing thing every time does that perfectly. Diversity is something we want and never asked for.</p>
<p>Trained on ten kinds of digit, a collapsed generator produces one kind — and its loss looks fine, because the loss only measures whether the discriminator was fooled.</p>
<p>The discriminator is supposed to punish this eventually, by learning that this exact output is always fake. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the generator simply moves to a different single output, and the two chase each other around the space forever without covering it.</p>
<h2 id="keeping-it-balanced"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#keeping-it-balanced">Keeping it balanced</a></h2>
<p>Everything above is a symptom of the same thing: two networks that only work while they are matched.</p>
<p>If the discriminator gets too strong, its loss goes to zero and the generator has no signal. If the generator gets too strong, the discriminator's judgement becomes uninformative. Neither can be allowed to win, which is an unusual property for a training procedure and the reason GAN work involves so much tuning of learning rates and update ratios.</p>
<p>The later variants mostly attack this. Convolutional architectures made image GANs stable enough to be practical. Conditioning added control over what gets generated. Style-based designs separated coarse structure from fine detail. Cycle-consistent designs allowed translating between two domains with no paired examples.</p>
<p>And the reason diffusion largely displaced GANs for image generation is not that it produces better images in principle — it is that its training has a fixed correct answer at every step and no opponent to balance against.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>A generator makes images from noise; a discriminator scores how real they look.</li>
<li>The generator never sees real data — only the discriminator's reaction.</li>
<li>Equilibrium is a discriminator scoring 0.5 on everything: losses of 1.3863 and 0.6931.</li>
<li>Lower is not better. A discriminator loss near zero means the run has failed.</li>
<li>Because both losses move against each other, neither is a reliable progress signal.</li>
<li>The original generator loss vanishes exactly when the generator is losing, so it is rewritten as <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mo>−</mo><mi>log</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mi>D</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">-\log D</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mop">lo<span style="margin-right:0.0139em;">g</span></span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0278em;">D</span></span></span></span>.</li>
<li>Mode collapse is the generator producing one convincing thing forever — the objective never asked for variety.</li>
<li>The whole method depends on neither network winning, which is why it is delicate.</li>
