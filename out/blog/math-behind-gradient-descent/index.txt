11:T9e57,<p>A model starts with weights that are wrong. Training is the process of nudging them until the error stops falling.</p>
<p>Gradient descent is how the nudging is decided. At each step it works out which direction is downhill for every weight, and moves a little that way.</p>
<h2 id="measuring-how-wrong-it-is"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#measuring-how-wrong-it-is">Measuring how wrong it is</a></h2>
<p>You cannot minimise an error you have not defined.</p>
<p>Say a model predicts a flat's rent as 46 and the real figure is 40. The error is 6. Square it and you get 36.</p>
<p>Squaring does two things. It removes the sign, so an overestimate and an underestimate both count as error rather than cancelling out. And it punishes big misses disproportionately — being wrong by 6 costs 36, but being wrong by 12 costs 144, four times as much for twice the error.</p>
<p>Average that over every example and you have the loss. One number saying how badly the model is doing. Everything that follows exists to make it smaller.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-gradient-tells-you"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-the-gradient-tells-you">What the gradient tells you</a></h2>
<p>The gradient of the loss with respect to a weight is its slope: how much the loss changes if that weight moves a fraction.</p>
<p>The sign is what matters.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Positive gradient</strong> — increasing the weight increases the loss. So decrease it.</li>
<li><strong>Negative gradient</strong> — increasing the weight decreases the loss. So increase it.</li>
<li><strong>Zero</strong> — flat ground. Nothing to gain by moving.</li>
</ul>
<p>The size matters too. A steep slope means the weight is far from where it should be, and a shallow one means it is close.</p>

<p>Both of those facts fit in one line:</p>

<h2 id="how-much-data-per-step"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-much-data-per-step">How much data per step</a></h2>
<p>The gradient is measured on examples, and how many you use per step is a real choice.</p>





























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>examples per step</th><th>speed</th><th>gradient</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>batch</td><td>all of them</td><td>slow</td><td>accurate</td></tr><tr><td>stochastic</td><td>one</td><td>fast</td><td>very noisy</td></tr><tr><td>mini-batch</td><td>32 to 1,024</td><td>good</td><td>good enough</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Using the whole dataset gives the truest direction and is far too slow to run often. Using one example is fast but the direction jumps around, since a single example is not representative.</p>
<p>Mini-batch sits between them and is what almost everything uses. The noise from a small sample turns out not to matter much — over many steps the errors average out, and the slight randomness even helps the weights escape shallow dips they would otherwise settle in.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>Loss is one number saying how wrong the model is; training minimises it.</li>
<li>The gradient of the loss with respect to a weight gives the direction and steepness of the slope.</li>
<li>The update rule is <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>w</mi><mo>←</mo><mi>w</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>α</mi><mtext> </mtext><mi mathvariant="normal">∂</mi><mi>L</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">∂</mi><mi>w</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">w \leftarrow w - \alpha \, \partial L / \partial w</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0269em;">w</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">←</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6667em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0269em;">w</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0037em;">α</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord" style="margin-right:0.0556em;">∂</span><span class="mord mathnormal">L</span><span class="mord">/</span><span class="mord" style="margin-right:0.0556em;">∂</span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0269em;">w</span></span></span></span>, and the subtraction handles direction on its own.</li>
<li>Steps shrink automatically as the gradient flattens near the minimum.</li>
<li>The learning rate multiplies the distance to the minimum by a fixed factor each step — too small crawls, too large explodes.</li>
<li>Every weight gets its own gradient and its own update, which is why the method scales to billions of them.</li>
<li>Mini-batches give a good enough gradient at a workable speed.</li>
