5:T41b1,<p>A model trained only to predict the next token is very good at continuing text and not at all interested in being useful. Ask it a question and a plausible continuation might be another question, or a page of links.</p>
<p>RLHF is how that becomes an assistant. The awkward part is that "helpful" has no formula, so it has to be learned from people — and the way people are asked matters more than it looks.</p>
<h2 id="ask-for-comparisons-not-scores"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#ask-for-comparisons-not-scores">Ask for comparisons, not scores</a></h2>
<p>Nobody can rate an answer 7.5 out of 10 consistently. Ask the same person the same question next week and the number moves; ask a second person and it moves more.</p>
<p>Ask instead which of two answers is better, and agreement is much higher. Comparison is a judgement people can actually make.</p>
<p>That leaves a problem: training needs a number, and comparisons are not numbers. The standard conversion assumes the probability of preferring A over B depends on the gap between two hidden scores:</p>

<p><strong>Supervised fine-tuning.</strong> Train the base model on tens of thousands of hand-written prompt-and-answer pairs. This does not make it good; it makes it <em>behave like an assistant</em> — answering when asked, stopping when finished. Everything after this needs a starting point that is already roughly in the right shape.</p>
<p><strong>The reward model.</strong> Take the fine-tuned model, generate several answers per prompt, have people say which they prefer, and fit the score above. The result is a model that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number predicting how a person would rank it. It is scaffolding — it is never deployed.</p>
<p><strong>Reinforcement learning.</strong> Generate answers, score them with the reward model, and nudge the weights toward higher scores. The nudges are deliberately small; large policy updates make this unstable.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-model-is-tethered"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-the-model-is-tethered">Why the model is tethered</a></h2>
<p>The reward model is an approximation fitted to a finite set of comparisons. Push hard enough on any approximation and you find the places it is wrong.</p>
<p>Left alone, that is exactly what happens. The model discovers that longer answers score well, or that confident phrasing scores well, or that refusing scores better than risking a bad answer — and it produces long, confident, hedging text that the reward model loves and nobody wants.</p>
<p>So a penalty is added for drifting away from the fine-tuned model it started as:</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mtext>objective</mtext><mo>=</mo><mtext>reward</mtext><mo>−</mo><mi>β</mi><mo>⋅</mo><mtext>KL</mtext><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mtext>model</mtext><mtext> </mtext><mi mathvariant="normal">∥</mi><mtext> </mtext><mtext>sft</mtext><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\text{objective} = \text{reward} - <p>Read the reward column on its own and the last row is best. Read the objective and it is the worst of the four.</p>
<p>The optimum is the second row — some movement, not much. That is the entire design: the reward is allowed to pull, and the penalty decides how far, and the best point is never the highest reward available.</p>
<h2 id="reward-hacking"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#reward-hacking">Reward hacking</a></h2>
<p>When it goes wrong it is recognisable.</p>
<p>Answers get longer without getting more informative. Every response opens with a paragraph of throat-clearing about how happy it is to help. Harmless questions get refused because refusal was safer in the preference data than a wrong answer.</p>
<p>None of these are bugs in the optimisation. It maximised what it was given, and what it was given was an imperfect model of what people wanted.</p>
<p>The defences are all about not trusting that model too far. Keep the penalty on. Re-collect preferences on the <em>current</em> model's outputs rather than the ones you started with, since old data cannot describe behaviours that did not exist yet. And keep a held-out human evaluation, because a rising reward score is not evidence of anything — the reward model is the thing being gamed.</p>
<h2 id="what-goes-wrong-in-practice"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-goes-wrong-in-practice">What goes wrong in practice</a></h2>
<p><strong>Skipping the fine-tuning stage.</strong> Reinforcement learning on a raw base model has almost nothing to reinforce; good answers are too rare to find by sampling.</p>
<p><strong>A weak reward model.</strong> It is the ceiling. The final model can only be as aligned as the thing scoring it.</p>
<p><strong>Turning off the penalty.</strong> Reliably produces drift into nonsense that scores well.</p>
<p><strong>Collecting preferences once.</strong> After a round of training the model produces different output, and the preference data describes a model that no longer exists.</p>
<p><strong>Inconsistent labellers.</strong> Two people applying different standards put contradictory signal into the same dataset, and the reward model learns the average of a disagreement.</p>
<p>There is also a simpler path. Instead of fitting a reward model and running reinforcement learning against it, the preference pairs can be used to update the model directly — which removes two models and most of the instability, at the cost of some of the flexibility.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>People rank pairs consistently and score individually badly, so preferences are collected as comparisons.</li>
<li>A score gap maps to a win rate through a sigmoid, and per-response scores can be recovered from comparisons alone.</li>
<li>Stage one teaches assistant behaviour; stage two fits the reward model; stage three optimises against it.</li>
<li>The reward model is scaffolding and is never shipped.</li>
<li>A KL penalty holds the model near where it started, and the best objective is never the highest reward.</li>
<li>Push too hard and you get reward hacking: long, over-polite, over-refusing answers that score well and help nobody.</li>
<li>Re-collect preferences each round, and judge progress by human evaluation rather than by the score being optimised.</li>
