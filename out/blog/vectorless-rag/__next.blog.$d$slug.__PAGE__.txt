5:T2610,<p>Ordinary RAG answers a question by turning it into a vector and fetching the chunks whose vectors are nearest. Vectorless RAG removes the vectors. The document is organised into a tree — chapters, sections, pages — and a language model reads its way down that tree, choosing a branch at each level, the way a person uses a table of contents.</p>
<p>No embedding model, no vector database, no chunking. The retrieval step is itself a sequence of reasoning calls.</p>
<p>It sounds like a regression. For a particular kind of document it is not.</p>
<h2 id="why-similarity-is-not-relevance"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-similarity-is-not-relevance">Why similarity is not relevance</a></h2>
<p>Ask a vector index over an annual report: <em>"What was operating margin in the third quarter?"</em></p>
<p>Every section that discusses margins is close to that question in embedding space — the CFO's letter, the risk factors, the segment analysis, last year's comparison, the glossary entry for "operating margin". The phrase that would actually decide the matter, <em>third quarter</em>, is a small part of the question's vector and an even smaller part of each chunk's.</p>
<p>The search returns five plausible chunks, ranked by how much they <em>sound like</em> the question. The one with the number may be sixth.</p>
<p>A person would not search this way. They would open the contents page, go to <em>Quarterly results</em>, then <em>Q3</em>, then the income statement. Each step uses the structure of the document, which an embedding throws away.</p>
<h2 id="how-the-navigation-works"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-the-navigation-works">How the navigation works</a></h2>
<p>The document is first turned into a tree, once, at indexing time. Each node stores a title and a short summary — a sentence or two of what is underneath it.</p>
<p>Take a 300-page report: 12 chapters, about 5 sections each, about 5 pages per section. At query time:</p>
<ol>
<li>The model reads the 12 chapter summaries and picks one.</li>
<li>It reads that chapter's 5 section summaries and picks one.</li>
<li>It reads that section's 5 page summaries and picks one or two.</li>
<li>It reads those pages in full and answers.</li>
</ol>
<p>With summaries of 60 tokens and pages of 500:</p>

































<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>tokens read</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>12 chapter summaries</td><td>720</td></tr><tr><td>5 section summaries</td><td>300</td></tr><tr><td>5 page summaries</td><td>300</td></tr><tr><td>2 full pages</td><td>1,000</td></tr><tr><td><strong>total</strong></td><td><strong>2,320</strong></td></tr><tr><td>the whole document</td><td>150,000</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>That is a <strong>65×</strong> reduction against stuffing the document into context, with no index other than the tree itself — and the tree is something many documents already have in their headings.</p>

<h2 id="where-it-breaks-the-errors-multiply"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-it-breaks-the-errors-multiply">Where it breaks: the errors multiply</a></h2>
<p>A vector search makes one decision — the ranking — and returns several candidates, so a near miss is often still in the top five.</p>
<p>Tree navigation makes one decision per level, and each one is final unless the system is built to back up. If the model picks the right branch with probability <em>p</em> at each level, it reaches the right page with probability <em>p</em> to the power of the depth:</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th>per-level accuracy</th><th>after 3 levels</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>98%</td><td>94.1%</td></tr><tr><td>95%</td><td><strong>85.7%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>90%</td><td>72.9%</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>A model that is right 19 times in 20 at every choice still loses one question in seven. The mitigations are the obvious ones: let the model pick two branches at the uncertain levels, let it back up when a page turns out not to contain the answer, and write summaries that state what a section <em>contains</em>, not what it is <em>called</em>.</p>
<p>The other costs are latency and money. Three or four sequential model calls before the answer starts is seconds, against tens of milliseconds for a vector lookup. Every query pays for reasoning that an embedding search did once at indexing time.</p>
<h2 id="when-it-is-the-right-tool"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#when-it-is-the-right-tool">When it is the right tool</a></h2>
<p><strong>Long, structured, high-stakes documents.</strong> Financial filings, contracts, regulations, technical manuals. They have real hierarchy, the questions reference that hierarchy ("in section 4.2", "for Q3", "under the indemnity clause"), and a wrong answer is expensive enough to justify a few seconds and a few cents.</p>
<p><strong>Questions where wording is shared across sections.</strong> When every chapter uses the same vocabulary, similarity has nothing to distinguish them by. Structure does.</p>
<p><strong>Small collections.</strong> Ten documents can each have a tree. A model can even choose the document first.</p>
<h2 id="when-it-is-not"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#when-it-is-not">When it is not</a></h2>
<p><strong>Many short, unstructured documents.</strong> A million support tickets have no table of contents. The top level of the tree would be a million summaries.</p>
<p><strong>Latency-sensitive paths.</strong> Autocomplete, chat over a knowledge base with a sub-second budget.</p>
<p><strong>Broad questions.</strong> "What are the main risks?" wants material from many branches at once — something a vector search returning many chunks handles naturally, and a single path through a tree does not.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Vectorless RAG swaps similarity search for navigation: the model reads summaries down a document tree and opens only the pages it chose. On a 300-page report that is about 2,320 tokens instead of 150,000, and it uses the one thing embeddings discard — the document's own structure.</p>
<p>The cost is that the search is now a chain of decisions, and accuracy is the product of them.
