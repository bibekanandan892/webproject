12:T21f9,<p>Kullback–Leibler divergence compares two probability distributions over the same outcomes. It turns up throughout machine learning: inside cross-entropy loss, in variational autoencoders, in knowledge distillation, and as the leash that keeps a model close to its original behaviour during reinforcement learning from human feedback.</p>
<p>It is easiest to understand as a cost.</p>
<h2 id="the-idea-bits-wasted-on-the-wrong-code"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-idea-bits-wasted-on-the-wrong-code">The idea: bits wasted on the wrong code</a></h2>
<p>Suppose events happen according to a true distribution <strong>P</strong>, but you design your expectations — a compression code, a prediction, a betting strategy — around a different distribution <strong>Q</strong>. Each outcome <em>x</em> costs <code>−log₂ Q(x)</code> bits under your code, when the best possible code would cost <code>−log₂ P(x)</code>. KL divergence is the average extra cost, weighted by how often each outcome really happens:</p>
<p><code>KL(P ‖ Q) = Σ P(x) · log₂( P(x) / Q(x) )</code></p>
<p>It is zero when Q equals P, and positive otherwise.</p>
<h2 id="step-by-step"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#step-by-step">Step by step</a></h2>
<p>Three outcomes. The truth is <strong>P = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]</strong>; the model believes <strong>Q = [0.4, 0.4, 0.2]</strong>.</p>













































<table><thead><tr><th>outcome</th><th>P</th><th>Q</th><th>P / Q</th><th>log₂(P/Q)</th><th>P · log₂(P/Q)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>A</td><td>0.5</td><td>0.4</td><td>1.25</td><td>0.3219</td><td>0.1610</td></tr><tr><td>B</td><td>0.3</td><td>0.4</td><td>0.75</td><td>−0.4150</td><td>−0.1245</td></tr><tr><td>C</td><td>0.2</td><td>0.2</td><td>1.00</td><td>0</td><td>0</td></tr><tr><td></td><td></td><td></td><td></td><td><strong>KL(P ‖ Q)</strong></td><td><strong>0.0365 bits</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Outcome A happens more often than Q expects, so it adds cost; B happens less often than expected, which partly offsets; C is predicted exactly. On average, using Q instead of P costs 0.0365 extra bits per outcome.</p>
<h2 id="it-is-not-a-distance"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#it-is-not-a-distance">It is not a distance</a></h2>
<p>Swap the roles — truth Q, belief P:</p>
<p><code>KL(Q ‖ P) = 0.0372 bits</code></p>
<p>Not the same number. KL divergence is <strong>asymmetric</strong>, so it is not a distance in the geometric sense, and the direction you compute it in matters. Here the two are close; for very different distributions they can differ enormously.</p>
<h2 id="where-it-blows-up"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-it-blows-up">Where it blows up</a></h2>
<p>The asymmetry is clearest when Q assigns almost no probability to something P considers likely. Keep P the same, but let the model believe <strong>Q = [0.5, 0.499999, 0.000001]</strong> — outcome C, which really happens 20% of the time, is treated as one in a million:</p>
<p><code>KL(P ‖ Q) = 3.301 bits</code></p>
<p>A hundred times larger. Each time C occurs, the code built on Q pays about 20 bits for it instead of 2.3. If Q assigned C exactly zero, the divergence would be infinite. <strong>KL(P ‖ Q) severely punishes Q for ruling out things that happen.</strong></p>
<p>The reverse direction behaves the other way: KL(Q ‖ P) barely cares if Q ignores outcomes, but punishes Q for putting probability where P has none. This difference shows up in practice:</p>
<ul>
<li>Minimising <strong>KL(P ‖ Q)</strong> over Q makes Q <em>cover</em> every outcome P allows — it spreads out. ("Mode-covering.")</li>
<li>Minimising <strong>KL(Q ‖ P)</strong> makes Q concentrate on a region P likes and ignore the rest. ("Mode-seeking.")</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="how-it-connects-to-cross-entropy"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-it-connects-to-cross-entropy">How it connects to cross-entropy</a></h2>
<p>Cross-entropy is the average cost of coding P's outcomes with Q's code: <code>H(P, Q) = −Σ P(x) log₂ Q(x)</code>. Entropy <code>H(P)</code> is the cost with the best code. KL is the difference:</p>
<p><code>H(P, Q) = H(P) + KL(P ‖ Q)</code></p>
<p>For the example: <code>1.4855 + 0.0365 = 1.5219</code> bits.</p>
<p>When training a classifier, P is the data's labels and is fixed, so H(P) is a constant. <strong>Minimising cross-entropy loss is exactly minimising KL divergence</strong> from the data to the model. Every classifier and language model trained with cross-entropy is, underneath, reducing a KL divergence.</p>
<p>KL from the uniform distribution has a neat reading too: <code>KL(P ‖ uniform) = log₂ n − H(P)</code>. For our P that is <code>log₂ 3 − 1.4855 = 0.0995</code> bits — how far P is from being completely uninformative.</p>
<h2 id="where-else-it-appears"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-else-it-appears">Where else it appears</a></h2>
<p><strong>RLHF and preference tuning.</strong> The reward-maximising policy is penalised by <code>β · KL(policy ‖ reference)</code>, so it cannot drift far from the pre-trained model while chasing reward. Without this, it finds degenerate outputs that score well and read badly.</p>
<p><strong>Knowledge distillation.</strong> A small student model is trained to minimise the KL divergence between its output distribution and a larger teacher's, learning from the teacher's full probabilities rather than just its top answers.</p>
<p><strong>Variational autoencoders.</strong> A KL term keeps each encoded distribution close to a simple prior, so the latent space stays smooth and can be sampled from.</p>
<p><strong>Monitoring.</strong> KL between the distribution of model inputs or outputs today and at launch is a simple drift alarm.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>KL divergence is the average number of extra bits paid for believing Q when P is true: <code>Σ P log(P/Q)</code>. For P = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2] and Q = [0.4, 0.4, 0.2] it is 0.0365 bits; reversed, 0.0372 — it is not a distance. It explodes when Q nearly rules out something P says is likely: 3.301 bits for a one-in-a-million belief in a 20% event.</p>
