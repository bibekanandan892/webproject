11:T1ea3,<p>In ordinary attention every position looks at every earlier position. A sequence of <em>n</em> tokens produces <em>n²</em> pairs of comparisons, which is fine at a thousand tokens and ruinous at a hundred thousand.</p>
<p>Sliding window attention limits each position to a fixed number of recent ones. A window of 4,096 means position 90,000 looks at positions 85,905 through 90,000 and nothing before that.</p>
<h2 id="what-that-saves-in-arithmetic"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-that-saves-in-arithmetic">What that saves in arithmetic</a></h2>





























<table><thead><tr><th>sequence length</th><th>full attention</th><th>windowed at 4,096</th><th>ratio</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>8,192</td><td>0.07B pairs</td><td>0.03B</td><td>2×</td></tr><tr><td>32,768</td><td>1.07B</td><td>0.13B</td><td>8×</td></tr><tr><td>131,072</td><td><strong>17.18B</strong></td><td><strong>0.54B</strong></td><td><strong>32×</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The cost stops being quadratic and becomes linear — <code>n × w</code> instead of <code>n²</code> — so the ratio is exactly <code>n / w</code> and grows without bound.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-saves-in-memory-which-matters-more"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-it-saves-in-memory-which-matters-more">What it saves in memory, which matters more</a></h2>
<p>The arithmetic is the advertised benefit. The memory is the one that decides what you can deploy.</p>
<p>With full attention, every generated token adds a set of keys and values that must be kept for the rest of the sequence. At roughly 128 KB per token for a mid-sized model:</p>





























<table><thead><tr><th>sequence length</th><th>full attention</th><th>windowed</th><th>ratio</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>8,192</td><td>1.07 GB</td><td>537 MB</td><td>2×</td></tr><tr><td>32,768</td><td>4.29 GB</td><td>537 MB</td><td>8×</td></tr><tr><td>131,072</td><td><strong>17.18 GB</strong></td><td><strong>537 MB</strong></td><td><strong>32×</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Look at the windowed column. It does not change. Past 4,096 tokens, positions falling out of the window are discarded, so the cache reaches a fixed size and stays there — <strong>a sequence of any length costs 537 MB.</strong></p>
<p>Turn that into concurrency. On 66 GB of free GPU memory at a 131,072-token context:</p>

















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>concurrent sequences</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>full attention</td><td><strong>3</strong></td></tr><tr><td>4,096-token window</td><td><strong>122</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Three requests becoming a hundred and twenty-two. That is not an efficiency improvement; it is the difference between a demo and a service.</p>

<h2 id="can-it-still-see-far"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#can-it-still-see-far">Can it still see far?</a></h2>
<p>Yes, indirectly, and the reach is exactly computable.</p>
<p>Layer 1 lets a token see 4,096 positions back. Layer 2's input at <em>that</em> position already summarises 4,096 before it, so layer 2 reaches 8,192. Stacking multiplies:</p>
<p><strong>receptive field = layers × window</strong></p>
<p>A 32-layer model with a 4,096 window can in principle propagate information across <strong>131,072 tokens</strong> — which is exactly the context length in the tables above, and not a coincidence.</p>
<p>But "in principle" is doing work. Information travelling <em>k</em> hops has been mixed with everything else at every hop. If each hop preserves a fraction of one specific signal:</p>



































<table><thead><tr><th>hops</th><th>signal remaining, r = 0.75</th><th>r = 0.6</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td>75.0%</td><td>60.0%</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td>56.3%</td><td>36.0%</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>31.6%</td><td>13.0%</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td><strong>10.0%</strong></td><td>1.7%</td></tr><tr><td>16</td><td>1.0%</td><td>0.03%</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Eight hops — 32,768 tokens away — arrives at 10% strength in the optimistic case and 1.7% in the pessimistic one. The path exists; it is not the same as looking directly.</p>
<p>So the honest statement is: a windowed model has a long <em>reach</em> and a short <em>grip</em>. Something 100,000 tokens back is reachable and will not be attended to sharply.</p>
<h2 id="which-is-why-it-is-usually-mixed"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#which-is-why-it-is-usually-mixed">Which is why it is usually mixed</a></h2>
<p>Few production models use a window everywhere. The common arrangement is to give most layers a window and a few layers full attention.</p>
<p>The full-attention layers provide the direct long-range paths — exact retrieval of a distant fact, with no hop attenuation. The windowed layers provide the cheap local mixing that most of the work actually needs. Since only a minority of layers keep a full cache, the memory stays close to the windowed figure.</p>
<p>That is a better design than either extreme, and it follows from the attenuation table: you need <em>some</em> direct paths, and you do not need thirty-two of them.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Two numbers, and they are the same number.</p>
<p>Windowing cuts attention cost by <code>n / w</code>, and it cuts the cache a sequence holds by the same factor — except the cache saving is better than it looks, because the windowed cache is <em>bounded</em> rather than merely smaller. Length stops being a variable in the memory budget.</p>
<p>What you give up is sharpness at distance, which decays with the number of hops rather than the number of tokens.
