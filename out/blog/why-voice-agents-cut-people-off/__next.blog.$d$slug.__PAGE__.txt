5:T1e16,<p>The most common complaint about voice agents is not that they give wrong answers. It is that they do not let people finish. The caller is mid-sentence, the agent starts talking, the caller stops, the agent stops, both start again. It feels rude, and it wrecks the conversation's rhythm far more than a slightly slow reply would.</p>
<h2 id="small-per-turn-rates-large-per-call-damage"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#small-per-turn-rates-large-per-call-damage">Small per-turn rates, large per-call damage</a></h2>
<p>Cut-offs are judged per turn by engineers and per call by users. The difference is large. If the agent interrupts on a fraction <em>p</em> of turns, the chance that a 12-turn call contains at least one interruption is <code>1 − (1 − p)¹²</code>:</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th>interrupted turns</th><th>calls with at least one interruption</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>2%</td><td>21.5%</td></tr><tr><td>5%</td><td><strong>46.0%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>10%</td><td><strong>71.8%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>A per-turn rate of 5% — which looks fine on a dashboard — means nearly half of all callers experience being talked over. To keep that below one call in five, the per-turn rate must be under about 2%.</p>
<h2 id="the-causes"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-causes">The causes</a></h2>
<p>Interruptions look identical from the caller's side but come from different places. Diagnosing them means finding which one is happening.</p>
<p><strong>1. Endpointing fires on a mid-turn pause.</strong> The caller pauses to think or breathe; silence passes the threshold; the agent replies. The most common cause, and worst for slow, hesitant or non-native speakers. <em>Fix:</em> model-based turn detection, adaptive thresholds per speaker, longer waits after incomplete phrases.</p>
<p><strong>2. Structured input is spoken in chunks.</strong> Phone numbers, card numbers, addresses and dates are read in groups with pauses between them. <em>Fix:</em> know what input is expected and wait until it is complete.</p>
<p><strong>3. The agent hears itself.</strong> Its own audio leaks back through the caller's microphone — speakerphones and laptops are the worst — and is mistaken for speech, triggering barge-in logic or confusing the turn state. <em>Fix:</em> echo cancellation, and higher detection thresholds while the agent is speaking.</p>
<p><strong>4. Backchannels treated as turns.</strong> The caller says "mm-hmm" or "yeah" while the agent speaks; the agent stops, then the system treats the "mm-hmm" as the caller's whole turn and replies to it. <em>Fix:</em> classify short acknowledgements as backchannels; keep talking.</p>
<p><strong>5. Transcription finalises too early.</strong> Some speech recognisers emit a "final" result after a short silence on their own schedule. If the agent acts on the recogniser's final rather than its own endpointing decision, it inherits the recogniser's shorter timeout. <em>Fix:</em> make one component own the endpoint decision.</p>
<p><strong>6. Speculation leaks.</strong> A speculative reply, generated early to save time, is played before endpointing has actually committed. <em>Fix:</em> speculate on generation, never on playback.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-find-which-cause-it-is"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-to-find-which-cause-it-is">How to find which cause it is</a></h2>
<p><strong>Label real interruptions.</strong> Pull recordings where the agent began speaking while the caller was still talking — detectable by overlapping speech on both channels — and listen to a sample.</p>
<p><strong>Classify each one</strong> by the list above. The pattern usually jumps out: interruptions clustered on number collection point at cause 2; on speakerphone calls at cause 3; right after the agent asks a question at cause 1.</p>
<p><strong>Look at the timeline per turn.</strong> VAD segments, recogniser partial and final events, the endpoint decision, the moment the first audio was sent. The order of these events tells you which component fired first.</p>
<p><strong>Track the per-call rate</strong>, not just the per-turn rate. It is the number that matches what users say about the product.</p>
<h2 id="recovering-when-it-still-happens"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#recovering-when-it-still-happens">Recovering when it still happens</a></h2>
<p>No system gets to zero. What matters next is recovery:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stop immediately</strong> when the caller keeps talking — within a couple of hundred milliseconds.</li>
<li><strong>Discard the interrupted reply</strong> from the conversation history, keeping only what was actually spoken.</li>
<li><strong>Merge the caller's speech</strong> before and after the interruption into one turn, so the model sees the full sentence.</li>
<li><strong>Do not apologise every time.</strong> A brief yield — or just silence and listening — is more natural than "Sorry, go ahead" after every overlap.</li>
<li><strong>Adapt.</strong> After interrupting a caller once, lengthen the wait for the rest of that call.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Voice agents cut people off for at least six distinct reasons: mid-turn pauses, chunked structured input, hearing their own echo, misreading backchannels, transcription finalising early, and speculative replies leaking out. Each has a different fix.</p>
<p>Measure it per call, not just per turn: 5% of turns is 46.0% of 12-turn calls.
