Filler phrases in voice agents

Sep 21, 2026/AI/4 min read

"Let me check that for you" is not politeness; it is latency engineering. If a lookup takes a median 800 ms with a 2.5 s tail, a 0.6 s filler covers it 33.9% of the time, a 1.2 s filler 72.2%, and a 2 s one 90.8% — and the remaining silences are the ones users notice.

People fill silence when they need time to think. "Hmm, let me see." "Good question." "One moment." Listeners hear these as natural, and they reset the clock: a pause after "let me check" feels like checking, not like a dropped call.

Voice agents borrow the trick deliberately. A filler phrase is short speech, played immediately, that covers work the agent has not finished yet — most often a tool call, sometimes a slow first token from the language model.

What a filler buys

The user's experience of latency is the silence they hear. A filler converts part of that silence into speech, and if the work finishes before the filler does, the silence disappears altogether.

Suppose the agent's lookups — checking an order, searching availability — take a median of 800 ms but have a long tail, with 5% taking 2.5 s or more. How often does a filler of a given length hide the whole lookup?

filler lengthexamplelookups fully covered
0.6 s"Okay."33.9%
1.2 s"Let me check that for you."72.2%
2.0 s"Sure, let me pull up your order details."90.8%

A one-second phrase hides most lookups completely. The rest still leave a gap — but a shorter one, after speech that explains it.

Longer fillers cover more, at a cost: they delay the answer on the lookups that were fast. If a lookup finishes in 300 ms, a 2-second filler makes the user wait 1.7 s longer than necessary. The filler should be roughly as long as the work it covers — and ideally chosen according to what is being done.

Two ways to produce them

Pre-recorded or pre-synthesised. A set of short clips, played instantly when a tool call starts. Zero synthesis delay, consistent quality — but they are fixed, and must be in the same voice as everything else or the change is obvious.

Generated by the model. The language model is prompted to say something before calling a tool, and that sentence is streamed to TTS as usual. Contextual — "Let me check Tuesday's availability" — and therefore more natural and more informative. But it costs a model turn's worth of latency before it can start, so it helps most when the tool is slow.

Many agents combine them: an instant short clip ("Sure —") the moment the user stops, then a model-generated, specific sentence while the tool runs.

Making them sound natural

Vary them. Hearing "Let me check that for you" five times in one call is grating. With a set of n interchangeable fillers chosen at random, the chance of the same one twice in a row is 1/n: 33% with three, 20% with five, 10% with ten. Avoid immediate repeats explicitly.

Make them specific when you can. "Checking your order now" tells the user the agent understood; "One moment" does not.

Do not lie. "I've found it!" before the result is known will be wrong sometimes. Fillers should describe what is happening, not what will happen.

Have a plan for the tail. If the tool is still running when the filler ends, the agent needs a second, different phrase — "Still looking, this one's taking a moment" — after a further second or two. Silence of three seconds after a filler is worse than silence without one, because it follows a promise.

Keep them interruptible. A user who says "never mind" during a filler should be able to stop it like any other speech.

Where fillers are the wrong answer

Fillers hide latency; they do not remove it. An agent that says "let me check" before every reply — including replies that need no checking — sounds evasive and wastes the user's time. Use them for genuinely slow operations, and spend engineering effort making the common path fast enough not to need one.

What to take away

A filler phrase plays speech while slow work finishes, turning silence into conversation. With lookups at a median of 800 ms and a 2.5 s tail, a 1.2 s filler hides 72.2% of them completely, a 2.0 s one 90.8%.

Match the filler's length to the work, prefer specific phrases over generic ones, vary them so they do not repeat, prepare a second phrase for the slow tail, and do not use them as a substitute for making the fast path fast.
