5:T1dd6,<p>Every voice agent has to decide when the user has finished speaking. There are two broad ways to do it.</p>
<p><strong>Silence-based</strong> detection waits for a fixed stretch of silence — say 500 ms — and declares the turn over. It looks only at the absence of sound.</p>
<p><strong>Model-based</strong> detection uses a model that looks at <em>what</em> has been said, and often <em>how</em>, to estimate the probability that the turn is complete. The silence threshold then depends on that estimate: short when the model is confident the user is done, long when it thinks they are mid-thought.</p>
<h2 id="why-silence-is-a-blunt-instrument"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-silence-is-a-blunt-instrument">Why silence is a blunt instrument</a></h2>
<p>Silence-based detection has one dial, and it is a bad trade at every setting. A short threshold interrupts people who pause to think; a long one makes every reply slow — including the replies to turns that were obviously over.</p>
<p>The waste is in that last part. "Yes, that's right." does not need 500 ms of silence to be recognised as finished. Neither does "Thanks, that's everything." A silence rule treats them exactly like "My account number is four, seven, two…", which plainly needs more time.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-turn-detection-model-sees"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-a-turn-detection-model-sees">What a turn-detection model sees</a></h2>
<p><strong>The words.</strong> A sentence ending in "and", "the", "my", "because", or in the middle of a number is unfinished. A complete answer to the agent's question — a date after "which day?", a yes after a yes/no question — is probably finished.</p>
<p><strong>The context.</strong> What the agent just asked shapes what a complete answer looks like.</p>
<p><strong>The prosody.</strong> Falling pitch and lengthened final syllables mark endings; level pitch and a cut-off mid-word mark a pause. Models that take audio as well as text can use these cues.</p>
<p>The model outputs a probability that the turn is over. The system maps that to a wait: for example, <strong>150 ms</strong> if the probability is above 0.8, <strong>1,000 ms</strong> otherwise.</p>
<h2 id="comparing-the-two"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#comparing-the-two">Comparing the two</a></h2>
<p>Using the same model of pauses as before — mid-turn pauses 85% short (averaging 120 ms) and 15% longer hesitations (averaging 600 ms), three per turn — and assuming the turn-detection model:</p>
<ul>
<li>is confident (above 0.8) on 70% of genuine endings, and</li>
<li>is wrongly confident on 5% of mid-turn pauses,</li>
</ul>
<p>the two policies compare like this:</p>























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>average wait after a real ending</th><th>mid-turn pauses cut off</th><th>turns cut off</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>silence, 500 ms</td><td>500 ms</td><td>7.8%</td><td><strong>21.7%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>model-based, 150 / 1,000 ms</td><td><strong>405 ms</strong></td><td>4.5%</td><td><strong>12.9%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The model-based policy is faster <em>and</em> interrupts less. It responds sooner on the 70% of endings that are clear, and waits longer on the uncertain pauses where a silence rule would have jumped in. Silence-only detection cannot do both, because it cannot tell those cases apart.</p>

<h2 id="what-model-based-detection-costs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-model-based-detection-costs">What model-based detection costs</a></h2>
<p><strong>Latency of its own.</strong> The model must run on every pause, quickly — tens of milliseconds. That rules out large models; turn detectors are small classifiers, often running on partial transcripts as they stream in.</p>
<p><strong>Dependence on the transcript.</strong> A text-based detector is only as good as the live transcription. If the recogniser has not yet emitted the last word, the detector judges an incomplete sentence. Audio-based detectors avoid this but are harder to build.</p>
<p><strong>Its own mistakes.</strong> In the example, 5% of mid-turn pauses fool the model into confidence, and those are cut off quickly. A confidently wrong detector can be worse than a cautious silence rule on specific patterns — so evaluate it on your own call types.</p>
<p><strong>Language and domain.</strong> What sounds finished differs across languages and tasks. A detector trained on English chat may misjudge Hindi, or a dictated address.</p>
<h2 id="using-both-together"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#using-both-together">Using both together</a></h2>
<p>In practice the approaches are layered:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>VAD</strong> still detects speech and silence frame by frame.</li>
<li><strong>The turn-detection model</strong> sets how long the silence must be, per pause.</li>
<li><strong>Hard bounds</strong> remain: never respond before a minimum (to avoid answering a breath), and always respond after a maximum (so a user who has walked away does not leave the agent waiting forever).</li>
<li><strong>Context overrides</strong> — extended waits after the agent asks for a number or an address — catch what the model misses.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Silence-based detection waits a fixed time after every pause, so it is equally slow on obvious endings and equally hasty on obvious mid-sentence pauses. Model-based detection reads the words, context and intonation, waits briefly when the turn is clearly over and longer when it is not.</p>
