<h2 id="the-two-axes"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-two-axes">The two axes</a></h2>
<p>Activations arrive as a grid: one row per example in the batch, one column per feature.</p>
<p><strong>Batch normalisation goes down a column.</strong> For each feature, take that feature's values across every example in the batch, and normalise using those.</p>
<p><strong>Layer normalisation goes across a row.</strong> For each example, take all of that example's features, and normalise using those.</p>

<h2 id="both-on-the-same-numbers"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#both-on-the-same-numbers">Both, on the same numbers</a></h2>
<p>Feature 2 in that grid sits around 40 while the others sit in single digits. Watch what each normalisation does about that.</p>
<p><strong>Batch norm</strong>, down each column:</p>

<p>Every column now has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Feature 2's original scale is gone — it competes on equal terms with the rest.</p>
<p><strong>Layer norm</strong>, across each row:</p>








































<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>f1</th><th>f2</th><th>f3</th><th>f4</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>ex 1</td><td>−0.69</td><td><strong>1.70</strong></td><td>−0.76</td><td>−0.25</td></tr><tr><td>ex 2</td><td>−0.50</td><td><strong>1.73</strong></td><td>−0.67</td><td>−0.56</td></tr><tr><td>ex 3</td><td>−0.60</td><td><strong>1.72</strong></td><td>−0.74</td><td>−0.38</td></tr><tr><td>ex 4</td><td>−0.41</td><td><strong>1.72</strong></td><td>−0.63</td><td>−0.68</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Every row now has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. But look at the second column — feature 2 is still the largest value in every single row, at almost exactly the same number each time.</p>
<p>That is the substantive difference, and it is easy to miss. Batch norm equalises features <em>against each other</em>. Layer norm does not; it only fixes the overall magnitude of each example, and any feature that is systematically larger than the others stays systematically larger.</p>
<p>Neither is wrong. They are answering different questions.</p>
<h2 id="why-sequence-models-cannot-use-batch-norm"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-sequence-models-cannot-use-batch-norm">Why sequence models cannot use batch norm</a></h2>
<p>Three problems, and they compound.</p>
<p><strong>It needs a batch.</strong> With a single example, a column contains one number, so its standard deviation is zero and the normalisation is undefined. Layer norm on that same single example works exactly as it always does — its statistics never involved the batch.</p>
<p><strong>It behaves differently at inference.</strong> During training the statistics come from the current batch; at inference there may be no batch, so a running average collected during training is substituted instead. Training and serving are therefore not the same computation, and small batches make those running averages noisy.</p>
<p><strong>Sequences vary in length.</strong> In a transformer the grid is not just examples by features — there is a position axis too, and different examples in a batch have different numbers of real positions. Averaging a feature "across the batch" means averaging across padding, or writing careful masking to avoid it.</p>
<p>Layer norm has none of these. It looks at one example's features and nothing else, so batch size, padding, and inference all become irrelevant.</p>
<h2 id="why-images-do-use-it"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-images-do-use-it">Why images do use it</a></h2>
<p>Convolutional networks on images have the opposite situation: large batches, fixed-size inputs, and channels that genuinely are comparable quantities across a dataset.</p>
<p>There, normalising a channel across the batch is meaningful, and it brings a real bonus — because each example's normalisation depends on whichever other examples happen to be in its batch, the output is slightly noisy, which acts as regularisation.</p>
<p>That noise is exactly what makes it unsuitable elsewhere. In a sequence model, an example's representation changing depending on what it was batched with is not a feature.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>Identical formula; the difference is which values the mean and variance come from.</li>
<li>Batch norm: one feature, across the examples in the batch. Layer norm: one example, across its features.</li>
<li>Batch norm makes features comparable to each other; layer norm only fixes each example's overall magnitude.</li>
<li>A feature on a larger scale stays larger under layer norm — it is normalised, not equalised.</li>
<li>Batch norm needs a batch, behaves differently at inference, and struggles with variable-length sequences.</li>
<li>Layer norm works at batch size 1 and is identical in training and serving, which is why transformers use it.</li>
<li>Batch norm's dependence on batch-mates is regularising for images and unacceptable for sequences.</li>
