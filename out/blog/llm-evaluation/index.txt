11:T1f4a,<p>Ordinary software has tests: run the function, compare against the expected value. Language model output has no expected value. There are many good answers to most questions and no way to enumerate them.</p>
<p>So evaluation stops being a correctness check and becomes a measurement problem, with a trade to make between how good the judgement is and how much of it you can afford.</p>
<h2 id="four-ways-to-measure"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#four-ways-to-measure">Four ways to measure</a></h2>
<p><strong>Formulas against a reference.</strong> Count overlapping words with a known-good answer, or measure embedding similarity to it. Instant and free. They also cannot tell a rephrasing from a contradiction — a correct answer using different words scores badly, and a wrong answer reusing the right vocabulary scores well. Useful where output is genuinely constrained; misleading almost everywhere else.</p>
<p><strong>Benchmarks.</strong> Standard datasets everyone runs, so numbers are comparable across models. Good for choosing between models, weak for telling you whether <em>your</em> application works, because your task is not in there.</p>
<p><strong>People.</strong> The only thing that actually knows what a good answer is. Slow, expensive, and inconsistent between raters, but it is the ground truth everything else is approximating.</p>
<p><strong>A model as the grader.</strong> A capable model scores the output against stated criteria. Understands meaning, runs at scale, and brings its own biases — toward length, toward confident phrasing, toward output from its own family.</p>
<h2 id="the-numbers-that-decide-the-design"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-numbers-that-decide-the-design">The numbers that decide the design</a></h2>
<p>Ten thousand outputs to evaluate:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>cost</th><th>wall time</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>formula</td><td>~$0</td><td>seconds</td></tr><tr><td>model as grader</td><td>$61.50</td><td>minutes, in parallel</td></tr><tr><td>people</td><td><strong>$12,500</strong></td><td><strong>500 person-hours</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Human evaluation is about <strong>200 times</strong> the cost of a model grader, and takes three months of one person's time instead of an afternoon.</p>
<p>That ratio is why nobody chooses one method. You grade everything with a model, and spend the human budget on a sample — used not to evaluate the output, but to check whether the grader can be trusted.</p>
<h2 id="how-big-a-sample"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-big-a-sample">How big a sample</a></h2>
<p>Which raises a question worth doing the arithmetic on. If people label a sample and you measure how often the grader agrees with them, how precisely do you know that agreement?</p>

<p>A hundred hand-labelled examples pins the agreement rate to about <strong>±7 points</strong>. That is enough to know the grader is roughly sane, and not enough to tell an 85% grader from an 80% one — for that you need around 400.</p>
<p>Worth knowing before someone reports that a prompt change improved agreement from 82% to 86% on a sample of fifty.</p>
<h2 id="the-two-failures-that-quietly-invalidate-everything"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-two-failures-that-quietly-invalidate-everything">The two failures that quietly invalidate everything</a></h2>
<p><strong>Contamination.</strong> If the test data was in the training data, the score measures memorisation. This is not a hypothetical for public benchmarks — they are on the web, and the web is the training set. A model can score well on a benchmark and fail on the same task phrased slightly differently, which is exactly the signature.</p>
<p>The defence is a private evaluation set drawn from your own data that has never been published.</p>
<p><strong>Drift.</strong> The model behind an API changes. Your prompts change. Retrieved documents change as the corpus grows. An evaluation run once at launch describes a system that no longer exists.</p>
<p>The defence is running the same set on a schedule and keeping the history, so a regression shows up as a step down in a chart rather than as a support ticket.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-working-setup-looks-like"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-a-working-setup-looks-like">What a working setup looks like</a></h2>
<p>A small evaluation set built from <strong>real cases</strong>, including the awkward ones — the ambiguous questions, the adversarial inputs, the rare-but-costly scenarios. A hundred well-chosen examples from your actual traffic are worth more than any public benchmark, because they are the distribution you serve.</p>
<p>Everything graded automatically, re-run on every change, with the results kept over time.</p>
<p>A human sample large enough to have validated the grader once, then re-checked occasionally.</p>
<p>And latency and cost tracked alongside quality, because a change that improves scores while doubling response time is not obviously an improvement, and nothing in a quality metric will tell you.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>Open-ended output has no expected value, so evaluation is measurement, not a correctness check.</li>
<li>Four families: reference formulas, benchmarks, people, and a model as grader.</li>
<li>People cost about 200× a model grader and take person-months rather than minutes.</li>
<li>So grade everything automatically and spend the human budget validating the grader.</li>
<li>100 hand-labelled examples pin agreement to ±7 points; distinguishing 85% from 80% needs about 400.</li>
<li>Contamination makes benchmark scores measure memorisation; a private set from your own data is the defence.</li>
<li>Drift makes any one-off evaluation stale; re-run on a schedule and keep the history.</li>
