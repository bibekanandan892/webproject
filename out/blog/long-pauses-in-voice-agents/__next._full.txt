12:T1f8c,<p>Most endpointing problems are about short pauses: the gap between clauses, a breath before the next word. Long pauses are a different situation. The user has not finished — they are <em>thinking</em>, or looking something up, or reading something off a screen — and the silence can last several seconds.</p>
<p><em>"What's your policy number?"</em> … <em>"Um, hang on, it's on the letter somewhere…"</em> … four seconds of rustling … <em>"Okay, it's P-X-4…"</em></p>
<p>An agent that treats that silence as the end of the turn either jumps in with "Sorry, I didn't catch that" or, worse, answers a question that has not been asked yet.</p>
<h2 id="how-long-thinking-pauses-are"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-long-thinking-pauses-are">How long thinking pauses are</a></h2>
<p>Suppose pauses of this kind — searching for information, recalling something, deciding — have a median of 2.5 seconds with a wide spread (a log-normal distribution). Then the share of them that outlast a given wait is:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>agent waits up to</th><th>thinking pauses that last longer</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1 s</td><td>87.4%</td></tr><tr><td>2 s</td><td><strong>61.0%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>4 s</td><td>27.8%</td></tr><tr><td>8 s</td><td>7.3%</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Endpointing tuned for ordinary turns — under a second — interrupts nearly all of them. Even a generous 2-second ceiling interrupts most. And raising the ceiling to 8 seconds to cover them would make every ordinary reply unbearably slow.</p>
<p>So long pauses cannot be handled by the timer. They have to be recognised.</p>
<h2 id="signs-the-user-is-holding-the-turn"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#signs-the-user-is-holding-the-turn">Signs the user is holding the turn</a></h2>
<p>People signal that they are keeping the floor, and those signals are detectable:</p>
<p><strong>Explicit holds.</strong> "Hang on." "Let me check." "One sec." "Let me think." These are as clear as a request can be.</p>
<p><strong>Fillers before silence.</strong> "Um…", "Uh…", "So…", "Well…" followed by a pause usually means the speaker is still composing.</p>
<p><strong>An incomplete answer.</strong> "My date of birth is…", or the first half of a policy number, is not a turn ending.</p>
<p><strong>The question asked.</strong> Requests for information the user may need to find — account numbers, dates, addresses, reference codes — invite long pauses. So do open questions like "What would you like to change?"</p>
<p><strong>Background activity.</strong> Typing, paper rustling or a muffled side conversation suggest the user is busy, not gone.</p>
<p>When these appear, the agent should switch from "waiting for the end of a turn" to "waiting for the user to come back": a much longer ceiling, and no reply until speech resumes or the ceiling is reached.</p>

<h2 id="what-the-agent-should-do-during-the-pause"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-the-agent-should-do-during-the-pause">What the agent should do during the pause</a></h2>
<p><strong>Nothing, at first.</strong> If the user said "hang on", silence is what they asked for. An agent that fills it with "Take your time!" every two seconds is worse than one that waits.</p>
<p><strong>One gentle check after a while.</strong> If the pause goes on — ten or fifteen seconds — a short, low-pressure prompt helps: <em>"No rush — I'm still here."</em> It confirms the line is open without demanding an answer.</p>
<p><strong>Offer help if they seem stuck.</strong> <em>"It's usually at the top of your renewal letter, starting with P-X."</em> This shortens the pause instead of just tolerating it.</p>
<p><strong>Offer a way out.</strong> For information the user may not have, say what else would work: <em>"If you can't find it, I can look you up by your postcode instead."</em></p>
<p><strong>Then close gracefully.</strong> Only after a long ceiling with no response should the agent assume something is wrong — and even then ask, rather than hang up: <em>"Are you still there?"</em></p>
<h2 id="dont-lose-the-partial-answer"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#dont-lose-the-partial-answer">Don't lose the partial answer</a></h2>
<p>Users often give part of an answer, pause, then continue: "It's P-X-4… [three seconds] …7-2-9." The agent must treat the whole as one answer. If endpointing fires during the pause, the transcript gets split into two turns, and the agent may respond to "P-X-4" as if it were complete.</p>
<p>Two guards help. First, after a partial structured answer — part of a number, an address with no postcode yet — keep the turn open longer. Second, if the user resumes immediately after the agent started replying, treat it as a barge-in and <em>merge</em> the new speech with the previous fragment rather than starting a fresh turn.</p>
<h2 id="prompting-the-model-for-it"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#prompting-the-model-for-it">Prompting the model for it</a></h2>
<p>Much of this behaviour can be shaped in the system prompt: tell the model that users may need time to find information, that "hang on" means wait without replying, and that a fragment of a number is not a full answer. Pair that with a turn-detection model that has seen hesitation patterns in its training data, and a long ceiling that activates on hold signals.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Long pauses are not slow endings; they are the user holding the turn while thinking or searching. With thinking pauses at a median of 2.5 s, a 2-second ceiling interrupts 61.0% of them and 4 seconds still interrupts 27.8% — so a longer global timer is not the answer.</p>
