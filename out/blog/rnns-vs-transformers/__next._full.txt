11:T2164,<p>Both architectures exist to read a sequence. They differ in one respect and everything else follows from it.</p>
<p>A <strong>recurrent network</strong> reads one position at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. Position 5 cannot be computed until position 4 is done.</p>
<p>A <strong>transformer</strong> reads every position at once, and each position uses attention to look directly at any other.</p>
<h2 id="what-sequential-reading-costs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-sequential-reading-costs">What sequential reading costs</a></h2>
<p>Consider a sentence where agreement depends on something far back:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>The letter, which had sat unopened in the drawer since the move, was —</p>
</blockquote>
<p>To choose the next word you need <em>letter</em>, which is eleven words earlier. A recurrent network has carried that information through eleven update steps, each of which mixed in new content and had a fixed-size state to do it in. Whatever survives of <em>letter</em> survived eleven rounds of compression.</p>
<p>A transformer's attention at the final position looks at <em>letter</em> directly. One hop, no decay, and the distance does not appear in the computation at all.</p>
<p>That is the capability argument, and it is real. But it is not why transformers took over.</p>
<h2 id="the-reason-they-won-training-depth"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-reason-they-won-training-depth">The reason they won: training depth</a></h2>
<p>Training processes whole sequences. The question is how many steps must happen <em>in order</em>.</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>sequence length</th><th>recurrent: sequential steps</th><th>transformer</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>128</td><td>128</td><td><strong>1</strong></td></tr><tr><td>512</td><td>512</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>2,048</td><td>2,048</td><td>1</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>A recurrent network cannot compute position 200 until 199 is finished, so a 2,048-token sequence has 2,048 dependencies to wait on regardless of how many processors are available. A transformer computes all positions in one pass.</p>
<p>On hardware whose entire advantage is doing thousands of independent things at once, that difference decides everything. The transformer is not better at using a GPU by a margin — it is the only one of the two that can use one properly.</p>
<h2 id="the-cost-crossover-exactly"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-cost-crossover-exactly">The cost crossover, exactly</a></h2>
<p>Per layer, ignoring constants:</p>
<ul>
<li>attention over <em>n</em> positions costs about <strong>2n²d</strong></li>
<li>a recurrent step repeated <em>n</em> times costs about <strong>2nd²</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>Set them equal: <code>n²d = nd²</code>, so <strong>n = d</strong>. For a hidden size of 768, the crossover is at exactly <strong>768 tokens</strong>.</p>















































<table><thead><tr><th>sequence length</th><th>transformer</th><th>recurrent</th><th>ratio</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>128</td><td>2.52 × 10⁷</td><td>1.51 × 10⁸</td><td><strong>0.17×</strong></td></tr><tr><td>512</td><td>4.03 × 10⁸</td><td>6.04 × 10⁸</td><td>0.67×</td></tr><tr><td><strong>768</strong></td><td>9.06 × 10⁸</td><td>9.06 × 10⁸</td><td><strong>1.00×</strong></td></tr><tr><td>2,048</td><td>6.44 × 10⁹</td><td>2.42 × 10⁹</td><td>2.67×</td></tr><tr><td>4,096</td><td>2.58 × 10¹⁰</td><td>4.83 × 10⁹</td><td>5.33×</td></tr><tr><td>16,384</td><td>4.12 × 10¹¹</td><td>1.93 × 10¹⁰</td><td><strong>21.33×</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Below the crossover the transformer does less arithmetic. Above it the recurrent network does — and the gap is exactly <code>n / d</code>, so at 16,384 tokens the transformer is doing 21× the work.</p>
<p>The transformer wins anyway at those lengths, because the work it does is parallel and the recurrent network's is not. Which is the whole trade in one sentence: <strong>transformers spend more arithmetic to remove the sequential dependency.</strong></p>

<h2 id="generation-flips-it"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#generation-flips-it">Generation flips it</a></h2>
<p>The picture at inference time is the reverse, and this is the part worth knowing.</p>
<p>To produce one more token with a history of <em>n</em>:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>cost</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>recurrent — update the state</td><td>1.18 × 10⁶, <strong>flat</strong></td></tr><tr><td>transformer at n = 512</td><td>7.86 × 10⁵ (0.7×)</td></tr><tr><td>transformer at n = 4,096</td><td>6.29 × 10⁶ (5.3×)</td></tr><tr><td>transformer at n = 32,768</td><td>5.03 × 10⁷ (<strong>42.7×</strong>)</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>A recurrent network's per-token cost does not depend on how much came before — the state is fixed-size, and generating the ten-thousandth token costs the same as the tenth. A transformer has to attend over everything it has written, so its per-token cost grows linearly with the history.</p>
<p>There is no parallelism to recover here, because generation is inherently sequential either way. So the transformer's advantage does not apply, and its cost disadvantage does.</p>
<p>That is why recurrent-style architectures keep being revisited for long-sequence and streaming work. The question they are answering is not "can we beat attention at reading" — it is "can we get a fixed-size state back without giving up trainability".</p>
<h2 id="where-each-one-belongs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-each-one-belongs">Where each one belongs</a></h2>
<p><strong>A transformer</strong> for anything trained on substantial data, and for anything where a distant dependency has to be exact. Which is most current work.</p>
<p><strong>A recurrent model</strong> where the sequence is unbounded or arrives as a stream, where memory is fixed and small, or where per-token inference cost has to be constant. Sensor streams, always-on audio, small devices.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Attention did not win by being cheaper. At 16,384 tokens it does 21 times the arithmetic.</p>
