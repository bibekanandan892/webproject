12:T1f30,<p>Endpointing decides when a user has finished speaking. The simplest rule waits for a fixed stretch of silence. Dynamic endpointing replaces the fixed number with one that changes — per speaker, per question, per moment in the conversation.</p>
<p>The case for it starts with an observation: a fixed silence rule is not actually fixed. It means something different for every person who talks to it.</p>
<h2 id="people-pause-at-different-rates"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#people-pause-at-different-rates">People pause at different rates</a></h2>
<p>Some people speak quickly with short gaps. Others speak slowly, pausing to think between phrases. The same person pauses more when reading out a number, answering a hard question, or speaking a second language.</p>
<p>Model a speaker's pauses within a turn as before — mostly short gaps, with occasional longer hesitations — and scale them by a factor <em>k</em> for that speaker: 0.7 for a quick talker, 1.0 for a typical one, 1.6 for a slow one. With three pauses per turn, a fixed 500 ms threshold cuts off:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>speaker</th><th>pause scale</th><th>fixed 500 ms: turns cut off</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>quick</td><td>0.7×</td><td>13.7%</td></tr><tr><td>typical</td><td>1.0×</td><td>21.7%</td></tr><tr><td>slow</td><td>1.6×</td><td><strong>39.0%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The slow speaker is interrupted in two turns out of five. The quick speaker is interrupted less — but waits 500 ms after every turn when 350 ms would have done. The rule is too impatient for one and too patient for the other.</p>
<h2 id="adapting-to-the-speaker"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#adapting-to-the-speaker">Adapting to the speaker</a></h2>
<p>Scale the threshold by the same factor — measure how long <em>this</em> person's pauses tend to be, and wait proportionally:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>speaker</th><th>adaptive threshold</th><th>turns cut off</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>quick</td><td>350 ms</td><td>21.7%</td></tr><tr><td>typical</td><td>500 ms</td><td>21.7%</td></tr><tr><td>slow</td><td>800 ms</td><td>21.7%</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Every speaker now gets the same interruption rate, and the quick speaker gets replies 150 ms sooner. The slow speaker waits longer for a reply — but stops being cut off nearly twice as often as everyone else.</p>
<p>In practice the factor is estimated as the call goes on: track the lengths of this caller's mid-turn pauses (pauses after which they kept talking), and set the threshold at a percentile of that distribution. Start from a population default and move toward the speaker's own pattern as evidence accumulates.</p>

<h2 id="adapting-to-the-moment"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#adapting-to-the-moment">Adapting to the moment</a></h2>
<p>Speaker rate is one signal. The conversation itself supplies others:</p>
<p><strong>What the agent just asked.</strong> After "Can I have your phone number?" or "What's the address?", users read out long strings with natural pauses between groups of digits or lines. Extend the wait. After "Shall I go ahead?", a one-word answer is expected; shorten it.</p>
<p><strong>What the user just said.</strong> A transcript ending in "and", "but", "so", "my", or partway through a number signals more to come. A complete sentence that answers the question signals the end. This is where dynamic endpointing shades into model-based turn detection.</p>
<p><strong>What happened last time.</strong> If the agent cut this user off a moment ago, it has direct evidence that its threshold is too short for them. Back off — and keep the longer wait for the rest of the call.</p>
<p><strong>Noise and channel.</strong> On a noisy line, VAD is less certain about what is silence; slightly longer waits avoid treating noise gaps as endings.</p>
<h2 id="keeping-it-bounded"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#keeping-it-bounded">Keeping it bounded</a></h2>
<p>Adaptation needs limits, or a few outliers will push it somewhere unhelpful:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A floor</strong>, perhaps 200 ms, so the agent never responds to a breath.</li>
<li><strong>A ceiling</strong>, perhaps 2 seconds, so a user who has stopped — or walked away — is not left in silence indefinitely.</li>
<li><strong>Slow adjustment</strong>, so one unusually long pause does not double the threshold for the rest of the call.</li>
<li><strong>Reset on context change</strong>: a longer wait for a card number should not persist once the card number is done.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="how-it-fits-with-the-rest"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-it-fits-with-the-rest">How it fits with the rest</a></h2>
<p>Dynamic endpointing is usually one layer in a stack:</p>
<ol>
<li>VAD detects speech and silence frame by frame.</li>
<li>A turn-detection model estimates whether the words so far form a complete turn.</li>
<li>Dynamic endpointing sets the silence threshold from that estimate, the speaker's own pause pattern, and the conversational context.</li>
<li>Barge-in handling recovers when the decision is still wrong.</li>
</ol>
<p>Each layer is simple. Together they replace one number that is wrong for most people with a wait that fits the person and the moment.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>A fixed silence threshold treats every speaker differently: 500 ms cuts off 13.7% of a quick talker's turns and 39.0% of a slow talker's. Scaling the threshold to each speaker's own pauses — 350 ms and 800 ms — gives both the same 21.7%, and gives the quick talker faster replies.</p>
