12:T288c,<p>Neural networks produce scores. A classifier produces one score per class, a language model one score per vocabulary token, an attention head one score per earlier position. The scores — <strong>logits</strong> — can be any real numbers, positive or negative, and do not add up to anything in particular.</p>
<p>Very often what is needed instead is a probability distribution: non-negative numbers that add up to 1. Softmax is the standard way to get one.</p>
<h2 id="the-formula"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-formula">The formula</a></h2>
<p>For scores <em>z₁ … zₙ</em>:</p>
<p><code>softmax(zᵢ) = e^(zᵢ) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ)</code></p>
<p>Exponentiate every score, then divide each by the total. Exponentiating makes everything positive; dividing makes it sum to 1.</p>
<p>Take three classes scored <code>[3.0, 1.0, 0.2]</code>:</p>





























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>score</th><th>e^score</th><th>probability</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>cat</td><td>3.0</td><td>20.09</td><td><strong>0.836</strong></td></tr><tr><td>dog</td><td>1.0</td><td>2.72</td><td>0.113</td></tr><tr><td>fox</td><td>0.2</td><td>1.22</td><td>0.051</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The ordering is preserved — the highest score gets the highest probability — but the exponential exaggerates gaps. A score lead of 2 becomes a probability ratio of e² ≈ 7.4. That is why it is called <em>soft</em>-max: it picks out the maximum, mostly, while still giving the others a share.</p>
<h2 id="only-differences-matter"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#only-differences-matter">Only differences matter</a></h2>
<p>Add the same constant to every score and the probabilities do not change. Adding 100:</p>
<p><code>[103.0, 101.0, 100.2]</code> → <code>[0.836, 0.113, 0.051]</code></p>
<p>The constant becomes a factor of <em>e¹⁰⁰</em> on top and bottom of the fraction, and cancels. Softmax depends only on how far apart the scores are, not on where they sit.</p>
<p>This is more than a curiosity; it is how softmax is computed safely.</p>
<h2 id="the-overflow-problem"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-overflow-problem">The overflow problem</a></h2>
<p>Exponentials grow absurdly fast. The largest number a 32-bit float can hold is about 3.4 × 10³⁸, which <em>e^x</em> exceeds at x ≈ <strong>88.7</strong>. In 16-bit floats — common in modern training and inference — the limit is 65,504, exceeded at x ≈ <strong>11.09</strong>.</p>
<p>Logits of 12 are entirely ordinary. A naive softmax in float16 would compute <code>e^12 = infinity</code>, divide infinity by infinity, and produce <code>NaN</code>, which then spreads through every calculation after it.</p>
<p>The fix uses the shift invariance: <strong>subtract the largest score first</strong>.</p>

<p>After the subtraction, the largest exponent is exactly 1 and every other one is between 0 and 1. Nothing can overflow, and the result is mathematically identical. Every framework's softmax does this internally — and so must any hand-written kernel, which is why Flash Attention tracks a running maximum as it processes attention scores in tiles.</p>

<h2 id="where-softmax-appears"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-softmax-appears">Where softmax appears</a></h2>
<p><strong>The output of a classifier.</strong> Scores per class become a predicted distribution, compared with the true label by cross-entropy loss.</p>
<p><strong>The output of a language model.</strong> Scores for every token in the vocabulary become the next-token distribution that sampling draws from. Temperature divides the logits before the softmax, sharpening or flattening it.</p>
<p><strong>Inside attention.</strong> Each query's scores against all keys go through a softmax to become attention weights — how much of each value to mix in.</p>
<h2 id="its-gradient-is-simple"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#its-gradient-is-simple">Its gradient is simple</a></h2>
<p>Paired with cross-entropy loss, softmax has an unusually clean gradient. For the example above, if the correct class is "cat", the gradient of the loss with respect to the scores is just <em>predicted minus target</em>:</p>
<p><code>[0.836 − 1, 0.113 − 0, 0.051 − 0] = [−0.164, 0.113, 0.051]</code></p>
<p>Push the correct class's score up by how far its probability fell short of 1; push each wrong class down by how much probability it took. That simplicity is one reason the pairing is universal.</p>
<h2 id="its-limits"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#its-limits">Its limits</a></h2>
<p>Softmax always produces a confident-looking distribution, even on inputs unlike anything the model was trained on. A probability of 0.84 is the model's score converted to a share, not a guarantee that it is right 84% of the time. Calibration has to be measured separately.</p>
<p>And softmax must give every option <em>some</em> probability — it can never output an exact zero. In attention, that means every position receives a little weight even when none is relevant, which is part of why attention sinks appear.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Softmax exponentiates each score and divides by the total, turning any list of logits into probabilities: <code>[3, 1, 0.2]</code> → <code>[0.836, 0.113, 0.051]</code>. It depends only on differences between scores, which is what makes the stable implementation possible — subtract the maximum before exponentiating, or float16 overflows at 11.09.</p>
