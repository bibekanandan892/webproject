5:T2229,<p>The terms come from communications. A <strong>half-duplex</strong> channel carries one direction at a time, like a walkie-talkie: one side talks, says "over", and the other replies. A <strong>full-duplex</strong> channel carries both directions at once, like a phone call.</p>
<p>Voice agents can be built either way, and the choice shapes how natural they can feel.</p>
<h2 id="half-duplex-taking-turns"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#half-duplex-taking-turns">Half-duplex: taking turns</a></h2>
<p>Most voice agents are, at heart, half-duplex. The conversation is a sequence of turns:</p>
<ol>
<li>Listen until the user has finished.</li>
<li>Think.</li>
<li>Speak until done — or until interrupted.</li>
<li>Repeat.</li>
</ol>
<p>Everything is organised around turn boundaries. Endpointing decides when a turn ends; barge-in logic decides when to abandon the agent's turn early. The language model sees a clean transcript of alternating turns, which is exactly the format it was trained on.</p>
<p>This is simple, debuggable, and works with any text LLM. Its limits all come from the turn structure:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Overlap is an error condition.</strong> If the user speaks while the agent is speaking, the system must decide: interruption (stop) or noise (ignore). Real conversation is full of overlap that is neither.</li>
<li><strong>Backchannels are awkward.</strong> "Mm-hmm" mid-sentence either halts the agent or is discarded; it is never simply <em>heard</em>.</li>
<li><strong>The agent cannot backchannel either.</strong> It cannot say "right" while the user talks, because it is not allowed to speak during their turn.</li>
<li><strong>Every decision is at a boundary.</strong> Latency depends on detecting the boundary, so the system is always waiting for silence.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="full-duplex-always-listening-sometimes-speaking"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#full-duplex-always-listening-sometimes-speaking">Full-duplex: always listening, sometimes speaking</a></h2>
<p>A full-duplex agent processes the incoming audio stream and its own outgoing stream continuously and simultaneously. There are no turns as such. At every moment — every frame, perhaps every 80 ms — the model decides what to output: silence, a backchannel, the start of a reply, or more of a reply in progress.</p>
<p>This is typically done with speech-native models that consume and produce audio tokens in parallel streams. The model learns turn-taking behaviour from data of real conversations, rather than having it imposed by endpointing rules.</p>
<p>What becomes possible:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Natural overlap.</strong> Starting a reply a fraction before the user quite finishes, as people do.</li>
<li><strong>Real backchannels.</strong> The agent can say "mm-hmm" or "right" while the user speaks, signalling it is following.</li>
<li><strong>Instant yielding.</strong> If the user cuts in, the model hears it in the very next frame and can stop — no separate barge-in pipeline.</li>
<li><strong>No silence threshold.</strong> The decision to speak comes from what was said and how, not from a timer.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="what-full-duplex-costs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-full-duplex-costs">What full-duplex costs</a></h2>
<p><strong>Compute that never stops.</strong> A half-duplex agent does heavy work around each turn. A full-duplex model runs on every frame, including silences. At 12.5 frames a second, a 10-minute call is <strong>7,500 model steps</strong>. A half-duplex agent producing twelve replies of about 78 tokens generates roughly <strong>936</strong> tokens of output. Even allowing for its transcription and prefill work, the always-on model costs several times as much per call — about 8× in steps alone.</p>
<p><strong>Control.</strong> There is no clean transcript between hearing and speaking for rules, tools and guardrails to act on. Production designs often pair a full-duplex speech front-end with a text model for reasoning and tool calls, which reintroduces some turn structure behind the scenes.</p>
<p><strong>Behaviour you have to train, not configure.</strong> How often to backchannel, when to overlap, how assertive to be — these become properties of the model learned from data. Adjusting them means retraining or fine-tuning, not changing a threshold.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluation.</strong> Overlap that is natural in one culture or context is rude in another. Measuring "good" turn-taking is harder than measuring latency.</p>
<h2 id="between-the-two"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#between-the-two">Between the two</a></h2>
<p>Most production systems in practice sit in between: a half-duplex core with full-duplex features bolted on.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Barge-in</strong> lets the user interrupt the agent's turn.</li>
<li><strong>Backchannel detection</strong> lets the agent keep talking through "mm-hmm".</li>
<li><strong>Speculative generation</strong> starts thinking before the turn boundary.</li>
<li><strong>Filler phrases</strong> let the agent take the turn before its answer is ready.</li>
</ul>
<p>Each approximates something full-duplex models do natively. Together they get much of the naturalness while keeping the text pipeline's control.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-choose"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-to-choose">How to choose</a></h2>
<p><strong>Half-duplex (with extensions)</strong> for task-oriented agents: customer service, booking, support, anything with tools, rules and audits. Turn structure is an asset there — it gives clean points to check, log and constrain.</p>
<p><strong>Full-duplex</strong> where conversational feel is the product: companions, language tutors, interactive characters, casual assistants. The cost and reduced control buy something users notice immediately.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Half-duplex agents take turns — listen, think, speak — and handle overlap as an exception through barge-in and backchannel rules. Full-duplex agents listen and speak at once, deciding every frame whether to talk, which makes overlap, backchannels and instant yielding natural.</p>
<p>The price is constant compute — about 7,500 model steps in a 10-minute call against roughly 936 generated tokens for a turn-based agent — and less room for explicit control.
