5:Ta66e,<p>Aligning a model to human preferences traditionally takes three pieces: a model that scores responses, a critic that predicts how good a state is, and a reinforcement learning algorithm to tie them together. Four networks in memory, and a training loop with several ways to go wrong.</p>
<p>DPO does the same job with one training step and no reward model at all. The reason it can is a piece of algebra worth seeing.</p>
<h2 id="the-reward-is-already-in-there"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-reward-is-already-in-there">The reward is already in there</a></h2>
<p>Start from what alignment is trying to do: maximise a reward while not drifting far from the model you started with.</p>

<p>Read that carefully. <strong>The reward is the log-ratio between the aligned model and the reference</strong>, plus a term that depends only on the prompt. A model that is aligned already encodes its reward function in how its probabilities differ from where it started. There is nothing left for a separate reward model to hold.</p>

<p>The third row is the problem. The model has made the <em>preferred</em> answer about 0.7% as likely as the reference model did — and satisfied the loss perfectly, because it pushed the rejected one down even harder.</p>
<p>This is not hypothetical; it is a known and observed behaviour. Pushing probability mass down is easier than pushing it up, so the optimisation often takes that route, and both responses lose likelihood while the margin looks healthy. Watching the loss fall tells you nothing about it. Watching the two log-ratios separately does.</p>
<h2 id="against-the-reinforcement-learning-route"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#against-the-reinforcement-learning-route">Against the reinforcement learning route</a></h2>



































<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>DPO</th><th>reward model + RL</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>networks in memory</td><td>2 (one frozen)</td><td>4</td></tr><tr><td>generation during training</td><td>none</td><td>yes, constantly</td></tr><tr><td>kind of algorithm</td><td>supervised</td><td>reinforcement learning</td></tr><tr><td>stability</td><td>ordinary gradient descent</td><td>needs care</td></tr><tr><td>what it can learn from</td><td>the pairs you have</td><td>anything it can generate and score</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The last row is the real trade. DPO learns from a fixed dataset of pairs, so it can only be as good as the pairs. The reinforcement learning route generates fresh responses from the current model, scores them, and learns from those — so it can discover behaviours nobody wrote down and keep improving as the model changes.</p>
<p>DPO's dataset is frozen at collection time. Once the model has moved past the responses in it, there is nothing more in there to learn.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>The optimal KL-constrained policy has a closed form, and inverting it shows the reward is the log-ratio to the reference model.</li>
<li>So a language model already encodes a reward function; no separate one is needed.</li>
<li>The intractable normaliser cancels in a pairwise comparison, which is exactly the shape preference data comes in.</li>
<li>The loss is <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mo>−</mo><mi>log</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mi>σ</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">-\log\sigma</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord">−</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mop">lo<span style="margin-right:0.0139em;">g</span></span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0359em;">σ</span></span></span></span> of the margin between the two log-ratios, and β sets how hard it pushes.</li>
<li>Two networks instead of four, no generation during training, and plain gradient descent.</li>
<li>The loss only sees the difference — both responses can drop in probability and it still looks satisfied.</li>
<li>Monitor the two log-ratios separately, not just the loss.</li>
