4:T2daf,<p>Open-ended output is hard to score. There is no single right answer to compare against, so the two traditional options are people, which does not scale, and word-overlap metrics, which measure whether the same words appeared rather than whether the answer was any good.</p>
<p>Using a model as the grader gets you judgement that understands meaning, at a rate that lets you evaluate everything rather than a sample.</p>
<h2 id="four-ways-to-ask"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#four-ways-to-ask">Four ways to ask</a></h2>
<p><strong>Score one answer.</strong> Rate it 1 to 5 on a criterion. Simple, and the least reliable — absolute scores drift between runs and cluster in the middle.</p>
<p><strong>Compare two answers.</strong> Show both, ask which is better. Far more stable, because relative judgements are easier than absolute ones. This is also where the worst bias lives.</p>
<p><strong>Compare against a reference.</strong> Show the model answer and a known-good one, and ask how close they are in substance. Needs ground truth, which you often do not have.</p>
<p><strong>Score against a rubric.</strong> Several named criteria, each scored separately, each with a stated definition. More work to set up, and the most usable in practice — a single number tells you something got worse, whereas a rubric tells you which part.</p>
<h2 id="the-bias-you-can-cancel"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-bias-you-can-cancel">The bias you can cancel</a></h2>

<h2 id="disagreement-is-information"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#disagreement-is-information">Disagreement is information</a></h2>
<p>There is a second thing the swap gives you free. When the two orders disagree, the judge did not have a real preference — position decided it.</p>

<p>So the flip rate across your evaluation set is a direct measure of how much the judge is actually distinguishing. If half your comparisons flip when swapped, the scores are noise dressed as measurement — and no amount of averaging fixes that, because there is nothing underneath.</p>
<p>Treating flipped comparisons as ties rather than forcing a winner is usually the honest thing to do.</p>
<h2 id="the-other-biases"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-other-biases">The other biases</a></h2>
<p>These do not cancel, and have to be designed around.</p>
<p><strong>Length.</strong> Longer answers score higher, roughly regardless of content. The cheapest partial defence is to state in the rubric that length is not a criterion, and to check whether your judge's scores correlate with word count — if they do, you have measured verbosity.</p>
<p><strong>Style.</strong> Bullet points, headings, confident phrasing. A well-formatted wrong answer beats a plainly-written right one more often than it should.</p>
<p><strong>Self-preference.</strong> A judge rates output from its own model family more highly. Which means evaluating your own model with a judge from the same family is not an independent measurement.</p>
<p><strong>Confidence.</strong> Hedged but correct loses to assertive but wrong. The judge is reading tone as evidence.</p>
<h2 id="making-it-usable"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#making-it-usable">Making it usable</a></h2>
<p><strong>The judge must be at least as strong as what it grades.</strong> A weaker judge cannot recognise the failures it is being asked to find, and will grade fluency instead.</p>
<p><strong>Ask for reasoning before the score, not after.</strong> A model that writes its verdict first and justifies it afterwards is producing a rationalisation. Reasoning first genuinely changes the score it lands on.</p>
<p><strong>Keep the scale short.</strong> 1–5 with each point defined beats 1–100, where nothing distinguishes 72 from 76 and the judge will not use the range consistently anyway.</p>
<p><strong>Validate against people once.</strong> Score a hundred examples by hand, run the judge on the same hundred, and check they agree. Without that, you have automated a measurement of unknown validity — and it will be trusted precisely because it produces numbers.</p>
<p><strong>Use more than one judge for anything important.</strong> Models from different families disagree in different directions, and the cases where they disagree with each other are the cases worth looking at.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>One model grades another's open-ended output, at a scale human review cannot reach.</li>
<li>Pairwise comparison is more reliable than absolute scoring; a rubric tells you <em>what</em> got worse.</li>
<li>Position bias is real and inflates the first-shown answer by a fixed amount.</li>
<li>Running both orders and averaging cancels it exactly.</li>
<li>The disagreement rate between orders measures how much the judge is really distinguishing.</li>
<li>Length, style, self-preference and confidence biases do not cancel and need designing around.</li>
<li>Reasoning before the score, a short scale, a judge at least as strong as the model, and one validation against humans.</li>
