12:T2327,<p>A model learns from examples, and it learns everything those examples make easy to learn — including things you did not intend. If every photo of a cat in the training set is centred and well lit, the model may learn that "cat" means "centred and well lit", and fail on a cat in the corner of a dark room.</p>
<p>Data augmentation fixes this by creating new training examples from existing ones, using transformations that change the input but not the correct answer. Each transformation tells the model: <em>this</em> does not matter.</p>
<h2 id="what-augmentations-say"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-augmentations-say">What augmentations say</a></h2>

































<table><thead><tr><th>transformation</th><th>what it teaches the model to ignore</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>shift / crop</td><td>where the object is in the frame</td></tr><tr><td>horizontal flip</td><td>left–right orientation</td></tr><tr><td>rotation (small)</td><td>camera tilt</td></tr><tr><td>brightness, contrast, colour</td><td>lighting conditions</td></tr><tr><td>blur, noise</td><td>camera quality</td></tr><tr><td>scale</td><td>distance to the object</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>These are <strong>invariances</strong>: properties the label should be independent of. Instead of hoping the model figures them out from enough natural variety, augmentation shows it the variety directly.</p>
<h2 id="how-many-examples-it-makes"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-many-examples-it-makes">How many examples it makes</a></h2>
<p>Take a classic setup: 32 × 32 images, padded by 4 pixels on each side and randomly cropped back to 32 × 32, plus a random horizontal flip.</p>
<ul>
<li>Crop positions: <code>(2 × 4 + 1)² = 81</code></li>
<li>Flips: 2</li>
<li><strong>Variants per image: 162</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>A dataset of 50,000 images becomes, in effect, <strong>8.1 million</strong> distinct training inputs. Combine further transformations — colour jitter, small rotations — and the space becomes practically unbounded.</p>
<p>In practice augmentations are applied randomly <strong>on the fly</strong>: each time an image is loaded during training, a fresh random transformation is drawn. No enlarged dataset is stored, and every epoch sees slightly different versions. That is why augmented models can train for many more epochs before overfitting.</p>

<h2 id="augmentations-can-lie"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#augmentations-can-lie">Augmentations can lie</a></h2>
<p>An augmentation is only valid if the correct label survives it. Some that are harmless for one task are wrong for another:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rotating a 6 by 180° makes a 9.</strong> Rotation augmentation for digit recognition must be limited to small angles.</li>
<li><strong>Flipping text</strong> turns it into mirror writing; flipping a road scene swaps which side traffic drives on.</li>
<li><strong>Cropping aggressively</strong> can remove the object entirely, leaving an image of background labelled "cat".</li>
<li><strong>Colour changes</strong> are wrong where colour <em>is</em> the label — ripe versus unripe fruit, a medical stain, a traffic light.</li>
</ul>
<p>Each of these teaches the model something false. Choosing augmentations is a statement about the task: which changes leave the answer alone.</p>
<h2 id="beyond-images"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#beyond-images">Beyond images</a></h2>
<p><strong>Audio and speech:</strong> adding background noise and room reverberation, changing speed and pitch slightly, masking short stretches of time or frequency. Speech recognisers trained this way cope far better with real phone lines.</p>
<p><strong>Text:</strong> harder, because small changes alter meaning. Common methods include <strong>back-translation</strong> (translate to another language and back to get a paraphrase), synonym replacement, and — increasingly — generating paraphrases or new examples with a language model. Each needs checking that the label still holds.</p>
<p><strong>Tabular data:</strong> usually least amenable; small noise on numeric features, or synthetic minority examples to balance rare classes.</p>
<p><strong>Mixing methods:</strong> <strong>mixup</strong> blends two images and their labels in proportion; <strong>cutmix</strong> pastes a patch of one image into another and mixes the labels by area. They do not preserve labels exactly — they create soft labels — and act as strong regularisers.</p>
<h2 id="when-it-helps-most"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#when-it-helps-most">When it helps most</a></h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Small datasets</strong>, where the model would otherwise memorise.</li>
<li><strong>Deployment conditions unlike the training data</strong> — different cameras, lighting, accents, noise.</li>
<li><strong>Long training runs</strong>, where repeated exposure to identical examples would overfit.</li>
</ul>
<p>For very large datasets it matters less, though even large vision and speech models use it. And it never replaces real data from the conditions you deploy in; it widens what you have, it does not cover what you lack.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Data augmentation creates new training examples by applying transformations that should not change the label, teaching the model which variations to ignore. Random 4-pixel crops and a flip give each 32 × 32 image 162 variants — 8.1 million inputs from 50,000 — drawn fresh every epoch.</p>
<p>Choose augmentations by asking whether the true answer survives them: a flip is harmless for a cat and wrong for text, a 180° rotation turns a 6 into a 9.
