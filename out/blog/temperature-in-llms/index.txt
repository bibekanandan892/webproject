12:T1f6b,<p>At every step a language model produces a score — a logit — for every token in its vocabulary. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and one token is drawn from them.</p>
<p>Temperature is a single number applied in between. The logits are divided by it before the softmax:</p>
<p><code>p(token) = softmax(logit / T)</code></p>
<p>That is all it does. Everything people describe as "creativity" or "randomness" is the consequence of that one division.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-division-does"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-the-division-does">What the division does</a></h2>
<p>Dividing by a number below 1 pushes the scores apart. Dividing by a number above 1 pulls them together. Since the softmax is exponential, small changes in the gaps become large changes in the probabilities.</p>
<p>Take the prompt <em>"The capital of France is"</em> and suppose the four top candidates score:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>token</th><th>logit</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Paris</td><td>4.0</td></tr><tr><td>the</td><td>3.0</td></tr><tr><td>a</td><td>2.0</td></tr><tr><td>Lyon</td><td>0.5</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>"the" and "a" are harmless — they lead to "the city of Paris" or "a city called Paris". "Lyon" is wrong.</p>













































<table><thead><tr><th>temperature</th><th>Paris</th><th>the</th><th>a</th><th>Lyon</th><th>entropy</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>0.2</td><td><strong>99.3%</strong></td><td>0.7%</td><td>0.0%</td><td>0.0%</td><td>0.06 bits</td></tr><tr><td>0.7</td><td>76.7%</td><td>18.4%</td><td>4.4%</td><td>0.5%</td><td>0.98 bits</td></tr><tr><td>1.0</td><td>65.2%</td><td>24.0%</td><td>8.8%</td><td>2.0%</td><td>1.32 bits</td></tr><tr><td>1.5</td><td>53.4%</td><td>27.4%</td><td>14.1%</td><td><strong>5.2%</strong></td><td>1.61 bits</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>At 1.0 the model's own distribution is used unchanged. At 0.2 it is almost deterministic: the top token takes 99.3%. At 1.5 the distribution is flattened enough that the wrong answer gets one draw in twenty.</p>
<p>As <em>T</em> approaches 0 the top token's share approaches 100% and sampling becomes greedy decoding — always take the highest logit. That is why "temperature 0" is the usual setting for reproducible output, although in practice tiny numerical differences on the hardware can still occasionally flip a near-tie.</p>

<h2 id="the-number-that-matters-at-scale"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-number-that-matters-at-scale">The number that matters: at scale</a></h2>
<p>5.2% sounds small. But a deployed model answers the same kind of question many times. The chance that "Lyon" appears at least once across 100 requests is <code>1 − (1 − p)^100</code>:</p>






























<table><thead><tr><th>temperature</th><th>P(Lyon) per request</th><th>at least once in 100 requests</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>0.2</td><td>~0.0%</td><td>0.0%</td></tr><tr><td>0.7</td><td>0.5%</td><td><strong>40.4%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>1.0</td><td>2.0%</td><td>86.3%</td></tr><tr><td>1.5</td><td>5.2%</td><td><strong>99.5%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>At the model's default temperature, a hundred users asking a factual question almost certainly produces at least one wrong answer — not because the model does not know, but because the sampler was told to sometimes pick a token the model considered unlikely.</p>
<p>And this is one token. A long answer makes hundreds of such draws. A low-probability token early on changes the context for every token after it, so the effect compounds over the length of the reply.</p>
<h2 id="choosing-a-temperature"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#choosing-a-temperature">Choosing a temperature</a></h2>
<p><strong>Facts, extraction, classification, code: low.</strong> 0 to 0.3. There is one right answer, the model's top choice is usually it, and variation is a defect.</p>
<p><strong>Writing, brainstorming, dialogue: moderate.</strong> 0.7 to 1.0. Here the top token is often bland — the most predictable word — and sampling from the rest produces more natural, varied text. Ten drafts that are all identical are useless.</p>
<p><strong>Above 1.0: rarely.</strong> The tail of the distribution is mostly tokens the model rated low for good reason. Text degrades into non sequiturs quickly.</p>
<p><strong>Sampling several answers to pick from:</strong> a higher temperature on purpose, because the point is to get answers that differ. Self-consistency — sampling many reasoning paths and taking the majority answer — relies on this.</p>
<p>Temperature is usually combined with top-k or top-p, which cut off the long tail entirely before sampling. That pairing makes moderate temperatures safer: the distribution is flattened only among tokens that were plausible to begin with.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Temperature divides the logits before the softmax. Below 1 it sharpens the distribution toward the top token; above 1 it flattens it toward the tail; at 0 it becomes greedy decoding.</p>
<p>The effect is easy to underestimate per token and hard to miss at scale: on the same four candidates, raising <em>T</em> from 0.2 to 1.5 takes the chance that a hundred users see the wrong answer at least once from zero to 99.5%.
