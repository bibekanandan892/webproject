12:T202e,<p>Endpointing is the decision that the user has finished speaking and it is the agent's turn. Every reply a voice agent gives starts with it, and every interruption it commits starts with it getting it wrong.</p>
<p>It helps to think of endpointing as what it actually is: a classifier. Each time the user goes quiet, it must answer one question — <em>is this the end of the turn, or a pause inside it?</em> — and it must answer quickly, because the time spent deciding is silence.</p>
<h2 id="the-machinery"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-machinery">The machinery</a></h2>
<p>A typical endpointing pipeline has three layers.</p>
<p><strong>Voice activity detection (VAD).</strong> The audio is cut into frames of 10–30 ms, and a small model scores each frame for the probability that it contains speech. VAD answers "is someone talking right now?", not "are they done?".</p>
<p><strong>Smoothing.</strong> Raw frame scores flicker. A short burst of non-speech in the middle of a word should not count as silence, and a single noisy frame should not count as speech. Smoothing — requiring several consecutive frames to change state, and holding "speech" for a short period after it ends — turns flickering scores into stable speech and silence segments.</p>
<p><strong>The decision rule.</strong> Given the current silence, decide whether the turn is over. The simplest rule is a timeout: <em>if silence has lasted T milliseconds, the turn has ended.</em> Better rules also consider what was said; that is model-based turn detection.</p>
<p>The speech-to-text system usually has its own idea of when an utterance is final, and the two must be coordinated. The agent should not act on a transcript that is still changing.</p>
<h2 id="endpointing-as-a-classifier"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#endpointing-as-a-classifier">Endpointing as a classifier</a></h2>
<p>Score it like one. Suppose a test set contains 1,000 genuine ends of turns and 3,000 pauses <em>within</em> turns — people pause mid-sentence often, typically several times per turn. With pauses modelled as 85% short (averaging 120 ms) and 15% longer hesitations (averaging 600 ms), a silence timeout of <em>T</em> behaves as follows:</p>

<p>At 300 ms, one firing in three is a mistake — the agent starts talking while the user is mid-sentence. At 1,200 ms, it is right 94% of the time, but every single reply now starts more than a second late.</p>
<p>Recall here is always 100% — every real ending eventually exceeds any timeout — so the only dial is how long to wait, and waiting trades directly against precision.</p>

<h2 id="how-to-evaluate-an-endpointer"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-to-evaluate-an-endpointer">How to evaluate an endpointer</a></h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Build a labelled set from real calls.</strong> Mark every pause longer than, say, 100 ms as <em>end of turn</em> or <em>mid-turn</em>. Include hesitations, lists, numbers and spelled-out names — the cases that break endpointers.</li>
<li><strong>Measure precision and latency together.</strong> For each configuration: how often it fires on a mid-turn pause, and how long after a real ending it fires (median and p95).</li>
<li><strong>Weight by consequence.</strong> Cutting someone off while they read a card number is worse than a slight delay after "thanks, bye". A single average hides that.</li>
<li><strong>Re-evaluate per channel.</strong> Phone audio, browser audio and noisy environments behave differently; VAD in particular degrades in noise.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="tuning-without-a-better-model"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#tuning-without-a-better-model">Tuning without a better model</a></h2>
<p>Within the silence-only approach, a few adjustments help:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Different timeouts for different moments.</strong> After the agent asks for a phone number, wait longer; after a yes/no question, wait less.</li>
<li><strong>Respect the transcript.</strong> If the last word is "and", "the", "my", or a number, the turn is almost certainly not over; extend the timeout.</li>
<li><strong>Adapt to the speaker.</strong> Someone who pauses a lot early in the call will keep doing it.</li>
</ul>
<p>Each of these is a step toward the real fix, which is to stop relying on silence alone: model-based turn detection, which reads the words and the intonation and predicts whether the turn is complete.</p>
<h2 id="recovering-from-mistakes"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#recovering-from-mistakes">Recovering from mistakes</a></h2>
<p>No endpointer is perfect, so the agent must handle its own errors:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>If it fires early and the user keeps talking,</strong> stop immediately (barge-in), discard the reply, and keep listening. The partial reply should not be recorded as said.</li>
<li><strong>If it fires late,</strong> there is nothing to recover — the user simply waited. This asymmetry is why many systems err slightly toward waiting.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Endpointing classifies each pause as the end of a turn or a pause within one, under time pressure. With silence alone, the only control is the timeout, and it trades precision against delay: over 1,000 endings and 3,000 mid-turn pauses, 300 ms is right 67.5% of the times it fires and 1,200 ms is right 94.2% — at the cost of 1.2 s before every reply.</p>
