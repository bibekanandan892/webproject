11:T1f82,<p>Any system that flags things gets four outcomes: correctly flagged, wrongly flagged, correctly passed, wrongly passed.</p>
<p><strong>Precision</strong> is how many of the things it flagged were right. <strong>Recall</strong> is how many of the things it should have flagged it found.</p>
<p>They pull against each other in the obvious way — a stricter threshold flags less and is right more often — and that is the version everyone learns. There is a bigger effect underneath it.</p>
<h2 id="what-rarity-does"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-rarity-does">What rarity does</a></h2>
<p>Take 100,000 transactions of which 0.2% are fraudulent. Two hundred real cases.</p>
<p>A classifier catching 95% of fraud with a 2% false-positive rate sounds strong. It catches 190 of the 200 — and flags 2% of the 99,800 clean transactions, which is 1,996 of them.</p>





















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>alerts raised</td><td>2,186</td></tr><tr><td>of which real</td><td>190</td></tr><tr><td><strong>precision</strong></td><td><strong>8.7%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Ninety-one percent of the alerts are wrong. Nothing about that classifier is bad; its recall is excellent and its false-positive rate is low. The problem is that <strong>there are 499 clean transactions for every fraudulent one</strong>, so even a small error rate on the clean ones buries the real cases.</p>
<h2 id="the-rule-this-implies"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-rule-this-implies">The rule this implies</a></h2>
<p>Tightening the false-positive rate, holding recall at 95%:</p>



































<table><thead><tr><th>false-positive rate</th><th>alerts</th><th>real</th><th>precision</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>2.0%</td><td>2,186</td><td>190</td><td>8.7%</td></tr><tr><td>0.5%</td><td>689</td><td>190</td><td>27.6%</td></tr><tr><td>0.2%</td><td>390</td><td>190</td><td>48.8%</td></tr><tr><td>0.1%</td><td>290</td><td>190</td><td><strong>65.6%</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Precision crosses 50% almost exactly where the false-positive rate crosses the prevalence — 0.190% against a prevalence of 0.2%.</p>
<p>That is the rule worth remembering: <strong>to get usable precision, the false-positive rate has to be smaller than the base rate.</strong> With fraud at one in 500, an error rate of one in 500 is the <em>threshold of usefulness</em>, not a good result.</p>
<p>It also explains a common frustration. Improving recall from 90% to 95% moves precision barely at all, because precision is dominated by the false-positive count. The lever that matters is the one on the negatives, and it is the harder one to move.</p>

<h2 id="deciding-without-pretending-it-is-symmetric"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#deciding-without-pretending-it-is-symmetric">Deciding, without pretending it is symmetric</a></h2>
<p>The standard advice — favour precision when false alarms are costly, recall when misses are — is correct and too vague to act on. The costs are usually knowable.</p>
<p>Say a missed fraud costs ₹18,000 and reviewing a false alert costs ₹150 of someone's time. The ratio is <strong>120:1</strong>, which means it is worth raising 120 false alerts to catch one more real case. That single number settles the threshold.</p>
<p>Total cost at four operating points:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>cost</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>recall 95%, fpr 2.0%</td><td>₹479,400</td></tr><tr><td>recall 95%, fpr 0.2%</td><td><strong>₹209,940</strong></td></tr><tr><td>recall 70%, fpr 0.2%</td><td>₹1,109,940</td></tr><tr><td>recall 50%, fpr 0.1%</td><td>₹1,814,970</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The best point is not the one with the best precision, nor the one with the best recall. It is the one with the lowest cost, and at a 120:1 ratio that means holding recall high and pushing the false-positive rate down — sacrificing recall is catastrophically expensive here, and a few hundred extra reviews barely register.</p>
<p>This also shows why the single-number summary that averages precision and recall is the wrong tool. It weights the two equally, which is true only when a miss and a false alarm cost the same. If you know the ratio, use the ratio; if you genuinely do not know it, that is the thing to go and find out.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>The tug of war between precision and recall is real and secondary.</p>
<p>The dominant effect is prevalence. When the thing you are looking for is rare, precision is governed by the error rate on everything else, and no amount of recall improvement helps.
