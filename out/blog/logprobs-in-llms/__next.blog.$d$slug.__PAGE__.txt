5:T1eb6,<p>When a language model writes a token, it first computes a probability for every token in its vocabulary, then picks one. The text you see keeps only the choice. <strong>Log probabilities</strong> — logprobs — keep the number: how likely the model considered each token it produced, and optionally the alternatives it considered.</p>
<p>Many APIs return them on request. They are one of the most useful and least used diagnostics a model offers.</p>
<h2 id="why-logs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-logs">Why logs?</a></h2>
<p>Probabilities of whole sequences are products of per-token probabilities, and products of many numbers below 1 underflow quickly — a 500-token answer's probability can be smaller than any float can represent. Logs turn products into sums, which stay well behaved:</p>
<p><code>log P(sequence) = Σ log P(token)</code></p>
<p>A logprob is always 0 or negative. 0 means certainty (probability 1). −0.1 is about 90%. −0.69 is 50%. −2.3 is 10%. −4.6 is 1%. More negative means less likely.</p>
<h2 id="a-worked-example"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#a-worked-example">A worked example</a></h2>
<p>Suppose a model writes four tokens with these probabilities:</p>



































<table><thead><tr><th>token</th><th>probability</th><th>logprob (natural log)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>"The"</td><td>0.90</td><td>−0.105</td></tr><tr><td>" meeting"</td><td>0.60</td><td>−0.511</td></tr><tr><td>" is"</td><td>0.95</td><td>−0.051</td></tr><tr><td>" Thursday"</td><td><strong>0.05</strong></td><td><strong>−2.996</strong></td></tr><tr><td><strong>sequence</strong></td><td><strong>0.0257</strong></td><td><strong>−3.663</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The total logprob is −3.663, so the model assigned this exact sequence a probability of about 2.6%.</p>
<p>More useful than the total is the shape. Three tokens are confident; one is not. The model was sure it was writing "The meeting is" and had only a 5% belief in "Thursday". If that day came from nowhere in the context, this is precisely where a hallucination would be. The text reads equally confidently throughout; the logprobs do not.</p>

<h2 id="perplexity"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#perplexity">Perplexity</a></h2>
<p>Averaging logprobs over a sequence and exponentiating the negative gives <strong>perplexity</strong>:</p>
<p><code>perplexity = exp(−mean logprob)</code></p>
<p>For the example, <code>exp(3.663 / 4) =</code> <strong>2.50</strong>. Read it as the effective number of equally likely choices the model was making at each step. A perplexity of 1 means total certainty; 2.5 means, on average, the model was choosing among two or three plausible tokens. Perplexity is the standard intrinsic measure of how well a language model fits a body of text: lower is better.</p>
<h2 id="what-logprobs-are-good-for"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-logprobs-are-good-for">What logprobs are good for</a></h2>
<p><strong>Confidence for classification.</strong> Ask a yes/no question and read the logprobs of "yes" and "no" rather than the text. If P(yes) = 0.82 and P(no) = 0.15, the normalised confidence is <code>0.82 / (0.82 + 0.15) = 0.845</code>. That is a usable score — thresholds, ranking, routing low-confidence cases to a human — instead of a binary string.</p>
<p><strong>Flagging likely hallucinations.</strong> Low-probability tokens inside names, numbers, dates and citations are where invented details tend to appear. Highlighting them, or checking them against a source, catches a large share of errors cheaply.</p>
<p><strong>Ranking and choosing.</strong> Score several candidate answers, or several retrieved passages, by the logprob the model assigns to them. Normalise by length, or long candidates lose by default.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluation.</strong> Multiple-choice benchmarks are often scored by which option has the highest logprob, avoiding the need to parse generated text.</p>
<p><strong>Detecting surprise.</strong> Text with unusually low logprobs under a model is unusual to it: useful for spotting out-of-distribution inputs, data quality problems, or text that the model did not itself write.</p>
<h2 id="caveats"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#caveats">Caveats</a></h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Logprobs are not calibrated by default.</strong> Instruction-tuned and preference-tuned models are often overconfident; a 0.95 token is not right 95% of the time. Calibrate on labelled data before treating them as probabilities.</li>
<li><strong>They reflect the sampling settings only partly.</strong> APIs typically report the model's raw distribution, before temperature or top-p filtering changed which token was actually picked.</li>
<li><strong>Tokenisation splits meaning.</strong> A name split into three tokens has three logprobs; the uncertainty may sit in the first piece and the rest follow confidently once it is chosen.</li>
<li><strong>Not every model or API exposes them</strong>, and reasoning models may expose them only for the final answer.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>Logprobs are the log of the probability the model assigned to each token it produced. Summing them gives a sequence's log-probability — −3.663 for four tokens at 0.9, 0.6, 0.95 and 0.05, or 2.6% — and the exponentiated negative mean gives perplexity, 2.50.</p>
<p>Their real value is in showing where the model was unsure: the one token at −2.996 in an otherwise confident sentence.
