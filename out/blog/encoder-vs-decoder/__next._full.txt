12:T281b,<p>A transformer processes a sequence of tokens with attention: every position looks at other positions and gathers what it needs from them.</p>
<p>The only structural difference between an encoder and a decoder is <strong>which positions each one is allowed to look at.</strong> An encoder sees the whole sequence. A decoder sees only what came before.</p>
<p>That single restriction has consequences far beyond what it sounds like.</p>
<h2 id="why-seeing-both-sides-helps"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-seeing-both-sides-helps">Why seeing both sides helps</a></h2>
<p>Take a sentence where a word is ambiguous until later:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>She left the plant by the window.</p>
<p>She left the plant at six.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The first <em>plant</em> is a pot of leaves; the second is a factory. When the model reaches that word it has no way to know which — the disambiguating information arrives afterwards.</p>
<p>An encoder reads bidirectionally, so the representation it builds for <em>plant</em> incorporates <em>window</em> or <em>at six</em>. It is not predicting anything; it is producing a representation of text that already exists, and there is no reason to withhold part of it.</p>
<p>This is why encoders suit classification, retrieval, extraction, tagging — anything where the input is complete and the job is to characterise it.</p>
<h2 id="why-a-decoder-cannot-do-that"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-a-decoder-cannot-do-that">Why a decoder cannot do that</a></h2>
<p>A decoder's job is to produce the next token. If it could see the next token while predicting it, the task would be trivial and it would learn nothing.</p>
<p>So a causal mask blocks every position from attending to anything after itself. Position 7 sees positions 1 to 7 and nothing else.</p>
<p>That is the whole distinction. Same layers, same attention, one mask setting — and everything else follows from it.</p>
<h2 id="the-first-consequence-training-signal"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-first-consequence-training-signal">The first consequence: training signal</a></h2>
<p>This is the part that explains which architecture won, and it is arithmetic.</p>
<p>An encoder is trained by hiding some tokens and asking it to recover them. Hide too many and there is not enough context left; the conventional figure is around 15%. So <strong>only 15% of positions produce a learning signal</strong>; the other 85% are context.</p>
<p>A decoder predicts every position from its prefix. Every token is simultaneously an input and a target, so <strong>100% of positions produce a signal.</strong></p>




















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>prediction targets per token of corpus</th><th>over a 1-trillion-token corpus</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>masked encoder</td><td>0.15</td><td>150 billion</td></tr><tr><td>causal decoder</td><td>1.00</td><td><strong>1,000 billion</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><strong>6.67× more learning from the same text.</strong> When the binding constraint on model quality is how much data you can get through, an architecture that extracts nearly seven times the signal per token is not slightly better — it is on a different scaling curve.</p>

<h2 id="the-second-consequence-generation-can-be-incremental"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-second-consequence-generation-can-be-incremental">The second consequence: generation can be incremental</a></h2>
<p>A causal mask guarantees that a token's representation depends only on tokens before it. So once computed, it never changes.</p>
<p>That is precisely what makes the attention cache possible. Generate a token, keep its keys and values, and the next step only has to process the one new position.</p>
<p>Without that guarantee, generating 500 tokens means reprocessing the whole prefix at every step:</p>

















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>token-positions processed to generate 500 tokens</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>recompute the prefix each step</td><td>125,250</td></tr><tr><td>reuse past keys and values</td><td><strong>500</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>A 250× difference, and it is not an optimization you could add to a bidirectional model. In an encoder, every new token changes every earlier representation, so there is nothing stable to cache. Bidirectionality and incremental generation are mutually exclusive.</p>
<h2 id="the-three-shapes"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-three-shapes">The three shapes</a></h2>
<p><strong>Encoder-only.</strong> Bidirectional, no generation. Good at understanding a fixed input — classification, retrieval, extraction. Still the right choice when the output is a label or a vector rather than text.</p>
<p><strong>Decoder-only.</strong> Causal, generates. What nearly all current chat and coding models are.</p>
<p><strong>Encoder-decoder.</strong> An encoder reads the input bidirectionally, a decoder writes the output while attending to that reading. Natural for transforming one text into another — translation, summarisation — where the input is complete and the output is new.</p>
<p>The interesting question is why decoder-only models took over tasks the other two were designed for. Partly the training-signal arithmetic above. Partly that a model good enough at predicting text turns out to be able to <em>describe</em> a classification in words, so the separate architecture stopped being necessary — one model, prompted differently, covers what previously needed three.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>The difference is one line of masking, and it cascades.</p>
<p>Blocking the future costs an encoder's bidirectional view of ambiguous words.
