4:T6eb7,<p>Stack enough layers and the numbers flowing through them drift. They grow, or they shrink towards nothing, and training becomes unstable either way.</p>
<p>Normalisation is the fix: at points along the network, pull the values back into a sensible range. RMSNorm is a stripped-down version of how that is usually done.</p>
<h2 id="what-layer-normalisation-does"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-layer-normalisation-does">What layer normalisation does</a></h2>
<p>Layer normalisation takes a vector and performs two separate corrections.</p>
<p>First it <strong>recentres</strong>: subtract the mean, so the values sit around zero. Then it <strong>rescales</strong>: divide by the standard deviation, so the spread is consistent.</p>

<h2 id="dropping-half-of-it"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#dropping-half-of-it">Dropping half of it</a></h2>
<p>RMSNorm rests on a claim: of those two corrections, only the rescaling was doing much work.</p>
<p>So it keeps the scaling and throws the centring away. There is no mean to subtract, which means no variance to compute either — variance is defined around the mean. What replaces it is the root mean square, which measures magnitude directly, without reference to any centre:</p>

<h2 id="watching-both-run"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#watching-both-run">Watching both run</a></h2>
<p>Take a vector with a mean that is clearly not zero: <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>x</mi><mo>=</mo><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mn>3</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>5</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>7</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>2</mn><mo separator="true">,</mo><mn>0</mn><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">x = [3, -1, 5, 7, -2, 0]</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.4306em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">x</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mopen">[</span><span class="mord">3</span><span class="mpunct">,</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord">−</span><span class="mord">1</span><span class="mpunct">,</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord">5</span><span class="mpunct">,</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord">7</span><span class="mpunct">,</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord">−</span><span class="mord">2</span><span class="mpunct">,</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.1667em;"></span><span class="mord">0</span><span class="mclose">]</span></span></span></span>.</p>
<p>Its mean is 2, its standard deviation is 3.2660, and its RMS is 3.8297.</p>





























<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>result</th><th>mean</th><th>RMS</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>input</td><td>3, −1, 5, 7, −2, 0</td><td>2.0000</td><td>3.8297</td></tr><tr><td>LayerNorm</td><td>0.3062, −0.9186, 0.9186, 1.5309, −1.2247, −0.6124</td><td>0.0000</td><td>1.0000</td></tr><tr><td>RMSNorm</td><td>0.7833, −0.2611, 1.3056, 1.8278, −0.5222, 0.0000</td><td>0.5222</td><td>1.0000</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Both land the magnitude in exactly the same place — an RMS of 1. That is the part that stabilises training, and RMSNorm gets there without the mean.</p>
<p>Where they differ is the offset. LayerNorm has moved the whole vector so it straddles zero. RMSNorm has left it where it was, only smaller: the mean of 2 became 2 ÷ 3.8297 = 0.5222.</p>

<h2 id="why-leaving-the-offset-alone-is-fine"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-leaving-the-offset-alone-is-fine">Why leaving the offset alone is fine</a></h2>
<p>The obvious worry is that a drifting mean will cause the trouble normalisation was supposed to prevent.</p>
<p>In a transformer it mostly does not, because the mean is not left unattended. Every sub-layer is followed by a linear projection, and a linear layer can shift its output by whatever its bias says — so any offset the network actually minds can be corrected there, using parameters that already exist. Recentring inside the normalisation step was doing a job something else was already able to do.</p>
<p>What could not be handled elsewhere is the magnitude, since that is what runs away across dozens of layers. RMSNorm keeps exactly that.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-buys"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-it-buys">What it buys</a></h2>
<p>Not much per call, but the calls are everywhere.</p>
<p>Computing a mean and then a variance means two passes over the vector, and the second depends on the first. Root mean square needs one pass and one square root. That removes a dependency as well as some arithmetic, which matters more than the operation count suggests — it is one fewer point where the whole vector has to be reduced before anything can continue.</p>
<p>The parameters shrink too. For a model with <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>d</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>4096</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">d = 4096</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">d</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6444em;"></span><span class="mord">4096</span></span></span></span> and 32 layers, normalising before attention, before the feed-forward network, and once at the end:</p>

<p>That is a rounding error against billions of weights. The speed is the reason it was adopted, not the size — normalisation runs twice per layer for every token of every batch across a training run of trillions of tokens, and a cheaper version of something that frequent is worth having.</p>
<h2 id="where-it-sits"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-it-sits">Where it sits</a></h2>
<p>In current models the normalisation goes <em>before</em> each sub-layer rather than after it — once before attention, once before the feed-forward network, and once more at the end before the output projection.</p>
<p>The residual path skips around it. That is the arrangement that makes deep stacks trainable: the untouched signal flows straight through, while each sub-layer sees an input that has already been brought back to a stable magnitude.</p>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>Deep stacks need normalisation or their values explode or vanish.</li>
<li>LayerNorm recentres and rescales; RMSNorm only rescales.</li>
<li>It divides by the root mean square, which measures magnitude without needing a mean.</li>
<li>With no centring step there is no <span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>β</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\beta</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.8889em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0528em;">β</span></span></span></span>, so it has half the learned parameters.</li>
<li>On the same vector both reach an RMS of 1; RMSNorm just leaves the offset in place.</li>
<li>That is safe because the following linear layer's bias can shift the mean anyway.</li>
<li>One pass instead of two, twice a layer, for trillions of tokens — which is why it took over.</li>
