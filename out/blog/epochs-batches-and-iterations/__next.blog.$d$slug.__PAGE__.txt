5:T202c,<p>Training a model means showing it data and adjusting its weights to reduce error. Three terms describe how that showing is organised, and they are easy to mix up:</p>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>batch</strong> is the group of examples processed together before one weight update. Its size is the <strong>batch size</strong>.</li>
<li>An <strong>iteration</strong> (or <strong>step</strong>) is one update: process one batch, compute the gradient, change the weights.</li>
<li>An <strong>epoch</strong> is one complete pass over the training set.</li>
</ul>
<p>They are tied together by a single equation:</p>
<p><code>iterations per epoch = ⌈ dataset size ÷ batch size ⌉</code></p>
<h2 id="a-worked-example"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#a-worked-example">A worked example</a></h2>
<p>Take a dataset of 50,000 images.</p>





























<table><thead><tr><th>batch size</th><th>iterations per epoch</th><th>iterations in 10 epochs</th><th>gradient noise (relative)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>32</td><td>1,563</td><td><strong>15,630</strong></td><td>1.00</td></tr><tr><td>128</td><td>391</td><td>3,910</td><td>0.50</td></tr><tr><td>512</td><td>98</td><td><strong>980</strong></td><td>0.25</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>(50,000 ÷ 32 = 1,562.5, rounded up because the last, smaller batch still counts.)</p>
<p>Every row trains on exactly the same data the same number of times — ten epochs. What changes is how many updates the model gets and how reliable each one is.</p>
<h2 id="why-batches-at-all"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-batches-at-all">Why batches at all</a></h2>
<p><strong>One example at a time</strong> gives an update per example, but each gradient is computed from a single example and is very noisy: it points toward what would help <em>this</em> example, which may hurt others.</p>
<p><strong>The whole dataset at once</strong> gives the true average gradient, but only one update per pass, and the dataset may not fit in memory.</p>
<p><strong>Mini-batches</strong> sit between. The gradient of a batch is an estimate of the full-dataset gradient, and its noise falls with the square root of the batch size: a batch 16 times larger gives an estimate 4 times less noisy, as in the table. And hardware processes a batch of examples almost as quickly as one, because of how matrix multiplication reuses data — so larger batches also make each step efficient.</p>

<h2 id="choosing-a-batch-size"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#choosing-a-batch-size">Choosing a batch size</a></h2>
<p><strong>Memory sets the ceiling.</strong> Activations for every example in the batch must be held for the backward pass. When the batch you want does not fit, <strong>gradient accumulation</strong> processes several small batches, adds up their gradients, and applies one update — equivalent to a larger batch, at the cost of time.</p>
<p><strong>Learning rate must move with it.</strong> A larger batch gives a less noisy gradient, so the model can take larger steps. A common rule of thumb scales the learning rate up roughly in proportion to the batch size, usually with a warm-up period at the start of training.</p>
<p><strong>Very large batches can generalise worse.</strong> Some noise in the gradient helps training escape sharp, narrow minima that fit the training set but not new data. Past a certain point, larger batches give diminishing returns per example: you process more data per step without making proportionally more progress.</p>
<p>Typical values: tens to a few hundred for small vision or tabular models on one GPU; millions of tokens per step for large language models across thousands of GPUs.</p>
<h2 id="how-many-epochs"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#how-many-epochs">How many epochs</a></h2>
<p>Too few and the model has not learned what the data can teach — <strong>underfitting</strong>. Too many and it starts memorising the training set — <strong>overfitting</strong> — and validation error rises while training error keeps falling.</p>
<p>The standard practice is to watch the validation loss after each epoch and stop when it stops improving (<strong>early stopping</strong>), keeping the best checkpoint. Small datasets often need many epochs; data augmentation helps by making each epoch slightly different.</p>
<h2 id="large-language-models-break-the-pattern"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#large-language-models-break-the-pattern">Large language models break the pattern</a></h2>
<p>A large language model trained on 15 trillion tokens, in batches of about 4 million tokens, takes:</p>
<p><code>15 × 10¹² ÷ 4 × 10⁶ = 3,750,000 steps</code></p>
<p>and in that time sees most of its data <strong>once</strong> — less than one epoch. Some high-quality sources are repeated a few times; most text never is. At that scale, data is so plentiful that seeing new examples beats revisiting old ones, and overfitting to the training set is less of a concern than whether there is enough good data at all.</p>
<p>The vocabulary therefore shifts: language model training is described in tokens and steps, not epochs.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>A batch is the set of examples behind one update; an iteration is one update; an epoch is one pass through the data. Iterations per epoch is the dataset size divided by the batch size: 1,563 for 50,000 examples at batch 32, 98 at batch 512.</p>
<p>Larger batches give fewer, steadier and more hardware-efficient steps — four times less gradient noise for sixteen times the batch — and need a correspondingly larger learning rate.
