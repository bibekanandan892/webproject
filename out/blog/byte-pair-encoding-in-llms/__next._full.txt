11:T3619,<p>A language model does not read text. It reads numbers. So before anything else happens, the text has to be cut into pieces, and each piece looked up as a number. Those pieces are tokens, and deciding where to cut is the job of the tokenizer.</p>
<p>Byte pair encoding, or BPE, is how most modern models decide. It is worth understanding because the cuts it makes shape everything downstream.</p>
<h2 id="the-two-obvious-approaches-and-why-both-fail"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-two-obvious-approaches-and-why-both-fail">The two obvious approaches, and why both fail</a></h2>
<p><strong>Cut on words.</strong> Give every word in your training text its own number. This reads nicely, but it breaks the moment a word shows up that was not in the training text. A brand name, a typo, a new bit of slang — the model has no number for it, and the whole word becomes a single "unknown" placeholder. Every meaning it carried is gone.</p>
<p><strong>Cut on characters.</strong> Give every character its own number. Now nothing is ever unknown, because any word can be spelled out. But the sequences become enormous — a short sentence turns into hundreds of tokens, and each one carries almost no meaning on its own. That is slow, and it makes the model work harder to learn anything.</p>
<p>One approach can't handle new words. The other drowns in tiny pieces. BPE lands in between.</p>
<h2 id="what-bpe-does"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-bpe-does">What BPE does</a></h2>
<p>Start with every word split into individual characters. Then repeat one simple move: find the pair of neighbouring symbols that occurs most often across the whole text, and glue that pair into a single new symbol.</p>
<p>Do it once and you might learn <code>in</code>. Do it again and you might learn <code>ing</code>. Keep going and you build a vocabulary of pieces that are bigger than characters but smaller than words — and crucially, the common pieces get learned first, because they are the ones that occur most.</p>
<p>You stop when the vocabulary reaches whatever size you decided on. Real models use somewhere between about 32,000 and 256,000.</p>

<p>Eight tokens down to three, and the pieces it ended up with — <code>ing</code>, and <code>ing&#x3C;/w></code> for a word actually ending in <em>ing</em> — are exactly the ones that earned their place by being common.</p>
<h2 id="tokenizing-text-it-has-never-seen"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#tokenizing-text-it-has-never-seen">Tokenizing text it has never seen</a></h2>
<p>Here is the part that matters. The merges are saved, <strong>in the order they were learned</strong>. That ordered list is the tokenizer.</p>
<p>To tokenize new text, split it into characters and replay the list from the top. Not "find the longest matching piece" — replay, in order.</p>
<p>So take <code>walking</code>, a word that never appeared in the training text at all:</p>
<pre><code>w a l k i n g &#x3C;/w>      split into characters
w a l k in g &#x3C;/w>       rule 1:  i + n
w a l k ing &#x3C;/w>        rule 2:  in + g
w al k ing &#x3C;/w>         rule 3:  a + l
w alk ing &#x3C;/w>          rule 5:  al + k
w alk ing&#x3C;/w>           rule 6:  ing + &#x3C;/w>
</code></pre>
<p>Result: <code>w</code> <code>alk</code> <code>ing&#x3C;/w></code> — three tokens. No unknown placeholder anywhere. The model has never seen this word, but it recognises the <code>alk</code> it learned from <code>walk</code> and <code>talking</code>, and the <code>ing&#x3C;/w></code> ending it learned from <code>singing</code> and <code>talking</code>.</p>
<p>That is the whole trick. New words are not a special case; they are just words that need more pieces.</p>
<h2 id="why-this-won"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#why-this-won">Why this won</a></h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nothing is ever unknown.</strong> In the worst case a word falls back to characters, which are always in the vocabulary. There is no cliff.</li>
<li><strong>The vocabulary stays small.</strong> Tens of thousands of entries covers essentially any input, instead of one entry per word and still missing some.</li>
<li><strong>Common things stay whole.</strong> Frequent words end up as single tokens because their pairs merged early. Only rare words get broken up, which is the right trade.</li>
<li><strong>It does not care about language.</strong> The algorithm counts pairs of symbols. It has no notion of English, or of spaces, so it works on any script.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-short-version"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-short-version">The short version</a></h2>
<ul>
<li>Models read numbers, so text has to be cut into tokens first.</li>
<li>Word-level cutting cannot handle unseen words; character-level cutting makes sequences far too long.</li>
<li>BPE starts from characters and repeatedly merges the most common neighbouring pair.</li>
<li>It stops once the vocabulary hits a target size, typically 32k–256k.</li>
<li>The saved merges are replayed <strong>in learning order</strong> to tokenize new text.</li>
<li>Unseen words simply decompose into known pieces, so nothing is ever unknown.</li>
