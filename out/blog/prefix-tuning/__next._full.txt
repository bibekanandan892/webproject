12:T249e,<p>Adapting a large model to a task by retraining all of it is expensive to run and expensive to store — a full copy of the weights per task.</p>
<p>Prefix tuning freezes the model entirely and trains a small set of extra numbers that attention can look at. The interesting question is <em>where</em> those numbers enter, because that decides what they can do.</p>
<h2 id="not-words"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#not-words">Not words</a></h2>
<p>The prefix is not text. It is not tokens the tokenizer produced, and it does not correspond to anything you could type.</p>
<p>It is a set of learned <strong>keys and values</strong> injected directly into the attention computation. Real tokens produce their keys and values by projecting their embeddings; the prefix's are just parameters, optimised by gradient descent to whatever numbers make the task work.</p>
<p>They take part in attention as things to be <em>attended to</em>. Every real token can look at them. They do not look at anything themselves — there is no query for a prefix position, because a prefix position is not computing an output.</p>
<h2 id="where-and-why-the-count-works-out"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#where-and-why-the-count-works-out">Where, and why the count works out</a></h2>
<p>Here is the part worth deriving rather than memorising.</p>
<p><strong>Prompt tuning</strong> puts learned vectors at the input only — virtual tokens prepended to the embedded sequence. For 20 virtual tokens at hidden size 2,048:</p>
<pre><code>20 × 2,048 = 40,960 parameters
</code></pre>
<p><strong>Prefix tuning</strong> injects a key and a value at <em>every layer</em>. For 24 layers:</p>
<pre><code>20 × 2 × 2,048 × 24 = 1,966,080 parameters
</code></pre>





















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>parameters</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>prompt tuning</td><td>40,960</td></tr><tr><td>prefix tuning</td><td><strong>1,966,080</strong></td></tr><tr><td>ratio</td><td><strong>48×</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>And 48 is not an empirical number. It is <code>2 × 24</code> — two for keys and values, twenty-four for the layers. <strong>The ratio between the two methods is exactly twice the depth of the model.</strong></p>
<p>That is also the answer to what the extra parameters buy. Prompt tuning's signal has to travel: it enters at layer 0 and whatever influence it has at layer 20 arrives through twenty layers of frozen computation that it cannot steer. Prefix tuning places parameters at layer 20 directly.</p>
<p>Against the whole model, the prefix is <strong>0.151%</strong> — 99.85% of the weights are frozen and shared.</p>

<h2 id="what-it-costs-at-inference"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-it-costs-at-inference">What it costs at inference</a></h2>
<p>This is the limitation that decided which method won in practice.</p>
<p>The prefix is present in every forward pass, forever. Twenty extra keys and values at every layer means every attention computation processes twenty more positions than it otherwise would:</p>

























<table><thead><tr><th>context length</th><th>extra attention work</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>100 tokens</td><td><strong>20.0%</strong></td></tr><tr><td>500</td><td>4.0%</td></tr><tr><td>2,000</td><td>1.0%</td></tr><tr><td>8,000</td><td>0.25%</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>The overhead is proportionally worst on short inputs, which is often exactly the workload — a classifier, a short extraction, a routing decision.</p>
<p>And twenty positions of the context window are gone. Not a large loss at 8,000, and material at 512.</p>
<p>Compare this with an approach that trains a low-rank update to the weight matrices: that update can be <strong>added into the weights</strong> after training, leaving a model that is exactly the original shape and exactly the original speed. Zero inference overhead, and the reason that approach became the default.</p>
<h2 id="what-it-is-good-at"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-it-is-good-at">What it is good at</a></h2>
<p>Storage, and it is a big win.</p>

















<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>per task</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>a full copy of the model</td><td>2.6 GB</td></tr><tr><td>one prefix</td><td><strong>3.93 MB</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>
<p><strong>661× smaller.</strong> Two hundred tasks cost 786 MB of prefixes against half a terabyte of model copies — and all two hundred share one loaded set of weights in memory, so switching tasks is swapping a few megabytes rather than reloading a model.</p>
<p>That is the shape of the argument for any of these methods: one frozen base, many tiny per-task files.</p>
<h2 id="the-honest-limitations"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#the-honest-limitations">The honest limitations</a></h2>
<p><strong>It is weaker than full fine-tuning</strong> on tasks that need a real change in capability. Adjusting what attention attends to is less expressive than adjusting the weights, and where the gap shows, it shows.</p>
<p><strong>Training can be unstable.</strong> The prefix parameters have no natural initialisation — they are not embeddings of anything — and poorly initialised prefixes train badly. Initialising from the embeddings of actual words is a common and slightly inelegant fix.</p>
<p><strong>The prefix is not interpretable.</strong> With prompt-based approaches you can at least decode the nearest real tokens and get a hint. A prefix's keys and values at layer 17 do not correspond to anything nameable.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away"><a class="heading-anchor" href="#what-to-take-away">What to take away</a></h2>
<p>The family of methods is distinguished by <em>where</em> the learned numbers enter: at the input, at every layer's attention, or in the weight matrices themselves.</p>
<p>Prefix tuning's choice — every layer's attention — is what makes it 48× larger than prompt tuning and more expressive for the same reason.
